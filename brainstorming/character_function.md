# CHARACTER FUNCTION FRAMEWORK

Use this framework to design, evaluate, and refine the major characters in a novel.

The purpose of this framework is to ensure that every important character has a **dramatic function**.

A character should not exist only because:

* the story "needs a friend";
* the protagonist needs someone to explain things to;
* the cast feels too small;
* the setting logically requires them;
* they have an interesting backstory;
* they represent a demographic or profession;
* they appear in a familiar genre role.

A strong character changes what can happen in the story.

They create:

* conflict;
* choices;
* emotional pressure;
* opportunities;
* information;
* temptation;
* complications;
* relationships;
* consequences.

The central question is:

> What does this character make possible that would not otherwise happen?

---

# 1. CHARACTER FUNCTION VS. CHARACTER TYPE

Do not confuse a character's identity with their dramatic function.

Examples of **character types**:

* detective;
* wizard;
* mother;
* soldier;
* teacher;
* prince;
* scientist;
* best friend.

These describe who someone is.

A **dramatic function** describes what they do for the story.

Examples:

* opponent;
* mentor;
* foil;
* rival;
* tempter;
* gatekeeper;
* confidant;
* emotional anchor;
* wildcard.

A wizard may function as:

* mentor;
* rival;
* antagonist;
* tempter;
* gatekeeper.

A mother may function as:

* ally;
* emotional stake;
* antagonist;
* moral authority;
* source of pressure.

Always identify function, not just role.

---

# 2. PRIMARY RULE

Every major character should do at least one of the following:

* help the protagonist;
* obstruct the protagonist;
* complicate the protagonist's choices;
* reveal something about the protagonist;
* challenge the protagonist's worldview;
* create emotional stakes;
* generate plot;
* provide access to something important;
* embody an alternative path;
* change because of the protagonist;
* force the protagonist to change.

The strongest supporting characters often do several of these at once.

---

# 3. THE PROTAGONIST

The protagonist is the character whose pursuit drives the central story.

The protagonist should:

* want something;
* make consequential choices;
* encounter sustained opposition;
* create later problems through earlier decisions;
* carry the central emotional or thematic arc.

Ask:

* What does the protagonist actively pursue?
* Why does this goal matter to them personally?
* What flaw, fear, belief, or contradiction makes pursuit harder?
* What decisions can only this protagonist make?
* Why is this story happening to this person?

Do not make the protagonist merely the person who witnesses the plot.

---

# 4. THE ANTAGONIST

The antagonist is the primary force whose goals or actions conflict with the protagonist's objective.

The antagonist should pursue something of their own.

They should not simply wait to block the protagonist.

Ask:

* What does the antagonist want?
* Why do they want it?
* What are they actively doing to achieve it?
* Why does their goal conflict with the protagonist?
* What resources or advantages do they possess?
* Why can't the protagonist simply avoid them?
* What does the antagonist believe that makes sense from their perspective?

A strong antagonist generates plot.

---

# 5. THE ALLY

The ally helps the protagonist pursue the external objective.

This help may involve:

* skills;
* knowledge;
* resources;
* emotional support;
* access;
* protection;
* influence.

An ally should not be endlessly agreeable.

Ask:

* Why are they helping?
* What do they want in return?
* What limits their loyalty?
* What happens when their priorities conflict with the protagonist's?
* What can they do that the protagonist cannot?

Strong allies have independent goals.

---

# 6. THE CONFIDANT

The confidant gives the protagonist someone with whom they can reveal thoughts, fears, plans, or emotional truths.

This function helps internal material become dramatic.

However, avoid creating a character whose only purpose is receiving exposition.

Ask:

* Why does the protagonist trust this person?
* What does the confidant want from the relationship?
* What happens when trust is tested?
* What information would the protagonist still refuse to share?
* Could the confidant also serve another function?

Often combine confidant with:

* ally;
* rival;
* love interest;
* sibling;
* mentor;
* foil.

---

# 7. THE MENTOR

The mentor provides:

* knowledge;
* training;
* worldview;
* strategy;
* access;
* experience.

A mentor becomes more interesting when their guidance is incomplete, flawed, biased, or costly.

Ask:

* What can the mentor teach?
* What can't they teach?
* What do they misunderstand?
* What does the protagonist eventually have to reject?
* What does the mentor want from the protagonist?
* Why can't the mentor solve the story themselves?

Avoid mentors who exist only to explain the world.

---

# 8. THE RIVAL

The rival pursues something comparable or incompatible with the protagonist's goal.

Rivals create:

* competition;
* comparison;
* jealousy;
* pressure;
* motivation;
* alternate paths.

Ask:

* What are they competing for?
* Why does losing to this rival matter?
* What does the rival do better than the protagonist?
* What does the rival reveal about the protagonist?
* Could rivalry become respect, friendship, or hatred?

Rivals are especially useful in:

* school stories;
* sports;
* romance;
* politics;
* career stories;
* tournaments;
* quests.

---

# 9. THE FOIL

A foil highlights qualities in another character through contrast.

The foil may embody:

* a different philosophy;
* a different reaction to similar trauma;
* an alternate future;
* the path the protagonist might have taken.

Ask:

* What does this character believe differently?
* What do they have in common with the protagonist?
* How do they make different choices under similar pressure?
* What does their outcome imply about the protagonist?

Foils are especially useful for theme.

---

# 10. THE TEMPTER

The tempter offers the protagonist something they want at a moral, emotional, or practical cost.

They may offer:

* power;
* safety;
* revenge;
* love;
* status;
* relief;
* belonging;
* forbidden knowledge.

Ask:

* What does the protagonist desperately want?
* What shortcut can the tempter offer?
* Why is the offer genuinely attractive?
* What would accepting it cost?
* Does the tempter believe their offer is reasonable?

A tempter does not have to be evil.

---

# 11. THE GATEKEEPER

The gatekeeper controls access to something the protagonist needs.

This may be:

* information;
* location;
* organization;
* relationship;
* resource;
* permission;
* opportunity.

Ask:

* What do they control?
* Why can't the protagonist bypass them?
* What does the gatekeeper want?
* What must the protagonist prove, sacrifice, or negotiate?
* Could the gatekeeper become an ally or antagonist later?

Gatekeepers turn access into conflict.

---

# 12. THE WILDCARD

The wildcard has independent goals and unpredictable loyalties.

They destabilize simple alliances.

A wildcard may:

* help one side temporarily;
* betray everyone;
* make choices based on self-interest;
* possess information nobody else has;
* alter the balance of power.

Ask:

* What do they want?
* Why doesn't that goal align cleanly with either side?
* What could cause them to change allegiance?
* What makes them useful?
* What makes them dangerous?

Do not make wildcards random.

Their behavior should be unpredictable to other characters but logically consistent with their own motives.

---

# 13. THE LOVE INTEREST

A love interest should function as more than romantic reward.

They should create:

* vulnerability;
* intimacy;
* competing priorities;
* emotional risk;
* character revelation;
* choices.

Ask:

* What draws these characters together?
* What keeps them apart?
* What does each person need from the relationship?
* How does the romance complicate the central plot?
* What worldview or future does the love interest represent?
* What does intimacy force the protagonist to confront?

The romance should alter the story.

---

# 14. THE EMOTIONAL ANCHOR

The emotional anchor represents what the protagonist is trying to protect, return to, or remain connected to.

Examples:

* child;
* sibling;
* spouse;
* friend;
* home;
* community.

This character often gives abstract stakes emotional weight.

Ask:

* Why does the protagonist care about them?
* What history makes the relationship specific?
* How can the central conflict strain the relationship?
* Does the anchor have desires of their own?

Avoid reducing them to a passive hostage.

---

# 15. THE MORAL AUTHORITY

This character represents a moral standard or worldview the protagonist respects.

They may:

* challenge rationalizations;
* confront hypocrisy;
* remind the protagonist what they stand for;
* expose moral compromise.

Ask:

* Why does the protagonist value their opinion?
* What happens if the protagonist disappoints them?
* Is the moral authority always correct?
* What might they fail to understand?

This character is strongest when they are human rather than infallible.

---

# 16. THE SHADOW SELF

The shadow character represents what the protagonist could become.

They often share:

* similar history;
* similar abilities;
* similar wounds;
* similar goals.

But they make different choices.

Ask:

* What do protagonist and shadow have in common?
* Where did their paths diverge?
* What does the shadow believe?
* What future does the shadow represent?

This function often works well for antagonists and rivals.

---

# 17. THE CATALYST

A catalyst pushes the protagonist into action.

They may:

* deliver information;
* make a demand;
* create a crisis;
* issue a challenge;
* commit an act that changes everything.

Catalysts often drive:

* inciting incidents;
* recruitment;
* discovery;
* confrontation.

Ask:

* What do they cause the protagonist to do?
* Why does their action matter?
* Are they still needed after initiating the story?

A catalyst may be a brief role or part of a larger function.

---

# 18. THE HERALD

The herald announces that change is coming.

This function is common in adventure, fantasy, and mythic structures.

A herald might be:

* messenger;
* survivor;
* stranger;
* warning;
* returning enemy;
* unexpected visitor.

Ask:

* What new possibility or danger do they reveal?
* Why does this information matter now?

A herald does not need to become a major character.

---

# 19. THE SOURCE OF INFORMATION

Some stories need characters with specialized knowledge.

Examples:

* scientist;
* detective contact;
* historian;
* hacker;
* doctor.

Avoid characters who exist only to explain things.

Strengthen them by adding another function.

For example:

The historian is also:

* protagonist's estranged father;
* rival scholar;
* suspected conspirator;
* gatekeeper.

Whenever possible:

> Information should come with relationship, conflict, or cost.

---

# 20. THE COMIC RELIEF FUNCTION

Humor can reduce tension, deepen affection, or highlight absurdity.

Do not create a character whose only defining quality is "funny."

Ask:

* Why are they funny?
* What worldview produces the humor?
* What serious function do they also perform?
* What happens when they stop joking?

Strong comic characters still matter when scenes become serious.

---

# 21. THE VICTIM / STAKES CHARACTER

Some characters demonstrate what is at risk.

They may:

* suffer from the antagonist's actions;
* show the consequences of failure;
* personalize a larger threat.

Avoid creating disposable characters solely to motivate the protagonist.

Ask:

* What does this character want?
* What relationship exists before harm occurs?
* Can they possess agency?
* Does their experience change the plot?

---

# 22. THE REPRESENTATIVE CHARACTER

Some characters embody a group, institution, culture, or faction.

Examples:

* soldier representing military perspective;
* union leader representing workers;
* noble representing ruling class.

Do not let one character become a simplistic spokesperson for an entire group.

Ask:

* What personal motives distinguish them?
* Where do they disagree with their own group?
* What contradiction exists between role and individual?

---

# 23. THE BRIDGE CHARACTER

The bridge character connects the protagonist to a world they cannot otherwise access.

Examples:

* outsider introducing protagonist to elite society;
* local guide;
* insider in criminal organization;
* member of another culture.

Ask:

* Why do they provide access?
* What do they gain?
* What limits their help?
* What allegiance complicates the relationship?

This function often combines well with gatekeeper or mentor.

---

# 24. THE DISRUPTOR

The disruptor destabilizes the status quo.

They may enter the story and immediately alter:

* relationships;
* hierarchy;
* plans;
* alliances;
* routines.

Ask:

* What changes because this person appears?
* Who benefits?
* Who feels threatened?
* Why can't everyone simply ignore them?

---

# 25. THE SECRET-KEEPER

This character possesses information that could alter the story.

Ask:

* What do they know?
* Why are they withholding it?
* What would happen if they revealed it?
* What does keeping the secret cost them?
* Is their silence believable?

Avoid arbitrary secrecy.

There should be a credible reason not to speak.

---

# 26. THE BETRAYER

A betrayal should emerge from character motive rather than exist only as a twist.

Ask:

* What does the betrayer want?
* When did their goals become incompatible?
* Why did the protagonist trust them?
* What warning signs existed?
* What do they gain or avoid by betraying the protagonist?

The strongest betrayals feel surprising in the moment but logical afterward.

---

# 27. THE CONVERT

The convert changes allegiance, worldview, or loyalty.

This may be:

* enemy to ally;
* skeptic to believer;
* loyalist to rebel;
* friend to opponent.

Ask:

* What experience changes them?
* What does conversion cost?
* How does their change affect the balance of power?

Conversions should be earned through events.

---

# 28. THE INNOCENT

An innocent character may reveal:

* what has not yet been corrupted;
* what others are fighting to protect;
* a simpler moral perspective.

Avoid making innocence synonymous with stupidity.

Ask:

* What does this person understand that more experienced characters overlook?
* What would losing their innocence mean?

---

# 29. THE PRAGMATIST

The pragmatist prioritizes effectiveness over ideals.

They create moral pressure by asking:

> What works?

They can challenge idealistic protagonists.

Ask:

* What compromise are they willing to make?
* Why?
* When does pragmatism become cruelty?
* When might they actually be right?

---

# 30. THE IDEALIST

The idealist prioritizes principle, belief, hope, or morality.

They may challenge cynical or pragmatic characters.

Ask:

* What principle will they not abandon?
* What does adherence cost?
* When does idealism become naïveté?
* When does idealism inspire others?

---

# 31. THE SKEPTIC

The skeptic questions plans, beliefs, assumptions, or supernatural explanations.

Useful for:

* preventing easy consensus;
* exposing weak logic;
* forcing protagonists to justify choices.

Ask:

* Why are they skeptical?
* What would convince them?
* What happens when they are wrong?
* What happens when they are right?

---

# 32. THE BELIEVER

The believer commits strongly to:

* religion;
* ideology;
* person;
* mission;
* prophecy;
* institution.

Their faith can create:

* courage;
* blindness;
* loyalty;
* sacrifice;
* conflict.

Ask:

* What do they believe?
* Why?
* What evidence could threaten that belief?
* What happens if the belief collapses?

---

# 33. THE SURVIVOR

The survivor has endured a version of the threat the protagonist now faces.

They can:

* warn;
* guide;
* traumatize;
* mislead;
* demonstrate consequences.

Ask:

* What did survival cost?
* What coping strategy did they develop?
* What do they misunderstand because of that experience?

---

# 34. THE AUTHORITY FIGURE

This character possesses formal power.

Examples:

* parent;
* commander;
* teacher;
* boss;
* ruler;
* judge.

Ask:

* What can they permit or forbid?
* What power do they actually have?
* What constrains them?
* How does the protagonist relate to authority?

Authority creates leverage.

---

# 35. THE DEPENDENT

Someone depends on the protagonist.

This can create:

* responsibility;
* emotional stakes;
* pressure;
* conflicting priorities.

Avoid making dependents helpless by default.

Ask:

* What can they contribute?
* What do they resent about dependence?
* What happens if the protagonist fails them?

---

# 36. THE BURDEN

A burden character creates practical or emotional difficulty for the protagonist.

Examples:

* injured companion;
* inexperienced recruit;
* political hostage.

Be careful not to make this function dehumanizing.

Ask:

* Why must the protagonist keep them close?
* What value do they provide?
* How might they reverse roles later?

---

# 37. THE CATALYTIC RELATIONSHIP

Sometimes the most important function is not individual.

A character exists because their relationship with the protagonist generates story.

Examples:

* estranged sibling;
* former lover;
* abusive mentor;
* competitive best friend;
* disappointed child.

Ask:

> What does this relationship force the protagonist to confront?

---

# 38. MULTI-FUNCTION CHARACTERS

The strongest casts often combine functions.

Examples:

### Mentor + Gatekeeper

The mentor can teach the protagonist but controls access to the organization they need.

### Love Interest + Rival

Attraction conflicts with competition.

### Confidant + Betrayer

The person who knows the protagonist best becomes capable of hurting them most.

### Ally + Foil

The ally helps externally while challenging the protagonist internally.

### Antagonist + Shadow Self

The villain represents the path the protagonist could take.

### Parent + Moral Authority + Emotional Stake

One relationship carries several dramatic functions.

Favor combinations when they deepen rather than confuse the character.

---

# 39. FUNCTION COLLISION

A particularly strong character performs functions that conflict with one another.

Example:

A mentor is also ordered to arrest the protagonist.

They want to:

* protect;
* teach;
* stop;
* obey.

This creates internal and interpersonal tension.

Ask:

> What happens when this character's functions become incompatible?

---

# 40. FUNCTION EVOLUTION

A character's role can change.

Examples:

Mentor → Opponent

Rival → Ally

Love Interest → Antagonist

Enemy → Confidant

Gatekeeper → Protector

Ally → Betrayer

These shifts can make relationships dynamic.

Ask:

* What causes the change?
* Is it earned?
* What does the change do to the protagonist?

---

# 41. STATIC FUNCTION WARNING

If a character performs exactly the same function throughout the entire novel, consider whether their role can evolve.

For example:

The best friend who:

* always agrees;
* always listens;
* always provides support;
* never wants anything;
* never creates problems.

This character may feel flat.

Give them:

* competing goals;
* limits;
* resentment;
* responsibility;
* secrets;
* changing loyalty.

---

# 42. CAST REDUNDANCY TEST

Two characters may be redundant when they:

* provide the same information;
* offer the same emotional support;
* hold the same worldview;
* create the same kind of conflict;
* perform the same plot function.

Ask:

> What would be lost if these characters were combined?

If the answer is very little, merge them.

Combining characters often improves:

* focus;
* relationships;
* emotional stakes;
* page economy.

---

# 43. CAST COMBINATION TEST

Whenever the cast becomes large, look for combinations.

Example:

Instead of:

* mentor;
* historian;
* estranged mother.

Consider:

> The protagonist's estranged mother is the historian who must teach them how to defeat the threat.

Now exposition carries emotional tension.

Another example:

Instead of:

* rival;
* love interest.

Consider:

> The romantic interest is competing for the same position.

One character produces more story.

---

# 44. CHARACTER REMOVAL TEST

For every major supporting character, ask:

> If this character disappeared, what would break?

Strong answer:

* protagonist loses access to information;
* emotional arc collapses;
* central relationship disappears;
* climax changes;
* opposition weakens;
* thematic contrast vanishes.

Weak answer:

> Someone else could do their scenes.

If the character is easily replaceable, strengthen, combine, or remove them.

---

# 45. CHARACTER REPLACEMENT TEST

Ask:

> Could any generic character perform this function?

Weak:

> A random guard gives the protagonist the key.

Stronger:

> The protagonist's estranged brother is the guard and must decide whether to betray his oath.

Whenever possible, give plot functions to characters who already carry emotional significance.

---

# 46. RELATIONSHIP DENSITY

A strong ensemble is interconnected.

Avoid structures where:

* every supporting character only knows the protagonist;
* supporting characters have no opinions about one another;
* every relationship serves the same emotional purpose.

Ask:

* Who likes whom?
* Who distrusts whom?
* Who needs whom?
* Who competes?
* Who has history?
* Who misunderstands whom?

The cast should feel like a network.

---

# 47. CHARACTER TRIANGLES

Triangles naturally create pressure.

Examples:

### Romantic Triangle

A wants B.
B wants C.
C needs A.

### Loyalty Triangle

Protagonist is loyal to both:

* family;
* organization.

Those two demand incompatible choices.

### Authority Triangle

Mentor and parent disagree about what the protagonist should become.

Triangles are often more dynamic than simple two-character conflicts.

---

# 48. CHARACTER AS PLOT GENERATOR

Each major character should be capable of causing events.

Ask:

> If this character pursued their goal aggressively for the next week, what would happen?

If the answer is "nothing," they may lack agency.

Even supporting characters should make decisions that create consequences.

---

# 49. INDEPENDENT MOTIVES

Every important character should want something that exists independently of helping or stopping the protagonist.

Weak:

> Her best friend just wants her to be happy.

Stronger:

> Her best friend needs the protagonist to stay in town because they are secretly relying on her income to keep the family business alive.

Independent motives produce conflict.

---

# 50. THE "WHAT DO THEY WANT FROM EACH OTHER?" TEST

For every major relationship identify:

### What A wants from B

and

### What B wants from A

These wants do not need to match.

Example:

A wants:

> forgiveness.

B wants:

> confession.

That mismatch generates scenes.

---

# 51. CONTRADICTION

Strong characters contain contradictions.

Examples:

* compassionate assassin;
* courageous soldier terrified of disappointing his father;
* cynical doctor who believes deeply in miracles;
* charming politician who hates being observed.

Contradictions should arise from character history or worldview rather than randomness.

Ask:

> What about this person does not fit the obvious stereotype?

---

# 52. STRENGTH AS WEAKNESS

Useful characters often possess qualities that help and hurt them.

Examples:

Loyalty:

* creates courage;
* makes betrayal difficult to recognize.

Intelligence:

* solves problems;
* produces arrogance.

Compassion:

* creates connection;
* makes difficult choices harder.

Discipline:

* creates competence;
* makes adaptation difficult.

Ask:

> When does this person's greatest strength become a liability?

---

# 53. PRESSURE RESPONSE

Know how each major character behaves under pressure.

Possible responses:

* attack;
* retreat;
* joke;
* control;
* appease;
* manipulate;
* freeze;
* intellectualize;
* take responsibility;
* blame others.

Different responses generate conflict.

Ask:

> What does this character do when frightened?

Often more useful than asking whether they are "brave."

---

# 54. BREAKING POINT

Identify what could force a major character to violate their normal behavior.

Examples:

A loyal soldier disobeys orders.

A pacifist attacks someone.

A liar finally tells the truth.

A loving parent abandons a child.

Ask:

> What would it take?

Breaking points create major turns.

---

# 55. CHARACTER SECRET

Secrets can create tension when they matter.

A useful secret should have consequences if revealed.

Ask:

* What is hidden?
* Why is it hidden?
* Who would be affected?
* What does keeping it cost?
* When would revelation hurt most?

Avoid secrets that only create surprise without changing relationships or goals.

---

# 56. CHARACTER LEVERAGE

Determine what can be used to influence each character.

Possible leverage:

* love;
* guilt;
* reputation;
* money;
* ambition;
* shame;
* loyalty;
* fear;
* debt;
* secrets.

Ask:

> What could make this character do something they normally would not do?

This is especially useful for antagonists and political stories.

---

# 57. CHARACTER VALUE SYSTEM

Know what each major character values most.

Examples:

* family;
* freedom;
* honor;
* safety;
* truth;
* loyalty;
* power;
* belonging;
* justice.

Then create conflicts where values collide.

Example:

Truth vs. loyalty.

Freedom vs. safety.

Justice vs. mercy.

These conflicts are often more interesting than good vs. evil.

---

# 58. FUNCTION AND THEME

Characters can embody different answers to the story's thematic question.

Example thematic question:

> Is security worth sacrificing freedom?

Characters might represent:

Protagonist:

> security first.

Friend:

> freedom first.

Antagonist:

> total control guarantees safety.

Mentor:

> freedom requires accepting risk.

Now character conflict also becomes thematic conflict.

---

# 59. FUNCTION AND WORLDVIEW

Each important character should have a recognizable way of interpreting events.

Ask:

* What do they believe about people?
* What do they believe about power?
* What do they believe about love?
* What do they believe about themselves?
* What assumption shapes their decisions?

This makes dialogue and choices distinct.

---

# 60. CHARACTER DIFFERENTIATION

Ensure major characters differ in several dimensions.

Compare:

* goals;
* methods;
* values;
* temperament;
* social status;
* relationship to authority;
* attitude toward risk;
* worldview;
* communication style.

Avoid casts where everyone sounds like the same intelligent, sarcastic person.

---

# 61. STATUS DYNAMICS

Relationships become more interesting when status can shift.

Status may come from:

* rank;
* wealth;
* competence;
* popularity;
* knowledge;
* power;
* emotional control.

Ask:

> Who has the upper hand at the beginning of the scene?

Then:

> Has that changed by the end?

Status movement creates drama.

---

# 62. CHARACTER ENTRANCE TEST

A major character's first appearance should ideally demonstrate something about their function.

Examples:

A rival beats the protagonist.

A gatekeeper refuses entry.

A mentor solves a problem the protagonist cannot.

A tempter offers exactly what the protagonist wants.

A wildcard disrupts an established plan.

Show function through action.

---

# 63. CHARACTER EXIT TEST

A major character's final appearance should reflect what changed.

Ask:

* Did the relationship transform?
* Did their allegiance change?
* Did they get what they wanted?
* Did they pay a cost?
* What does their ending demonstrate?

Avoid characters simply disappearing once their plot task is finished.

---

# 64. CHARACTER FUNCTION BY GENRE

Different genres often emphasize different functions.

### Mystery

Useful functions:

* investigator;
* victim;
* suspect;
* witness;
* confidant;
* rival investigator;
* gatekeeper;
* secret-keeper.

### Thriller

Useful functions:

* antagonist;
* target;
* ally;
* betrayer;
* authority;
* wildcard;
* informant.

### Romance

Useful functions:

* love interest;
* rival;
* confidant;
* family pressure;
* ex;
* foil;
* emotional anchor.

### Fantasy

Useful functions:

* mentor;
* gatekeeper;
* rival;
* faction representative;
* shadow self;
* tempter;
* companion.

### Horror

Useful functions:

* skeptic;
* believer;
* survivor;
* victim;
* authority;
* secret-keeper;
* tempter.

Do not mechanically fill genre slots.

Use only functions that generate the desired story.

---

# 65. ENSEMBLE BALANCE

For an ensemble, do not make every character equally important.

Identify:

### Tier 1 — Central

Characters essential to the core plot and emotional arc.

### Tier 2 — Major Supporting

Characters with recurring functions and meaningful arcs.

### Tier 3 — Secondary

Characters who serve narrower but necessary functions.

### Tier 4 — Minor

Characters used briefly for specific scenes or world texture.

This prevents overdevelopment of minor characters.

---

# 66. CHARACTER LOAD

Do not introduce more major characters than the story can meaningfully develop.

Ask:

* Can the reader remember this person?
* Do they have enough page time?
* Does their function justify that page time?
* Could their role be combined with someone else?

More characters do not automatically create a richer story.

---

# 67. NAMED CHARACTER TEST

Not every person needs a name.

Before naming a minor character, ask whether the reader will need to:

* remember them;
* understand their relationship;
* encounter them again;
* care about their fate.

Avoid clutter.

---

# 68. FUNCTION FIRST, BIOGRAPHY SECOND

When brainstorming a new character, use this order:

### Step 1: Function

What does the story need this character to do?

### Step 2: Goal

What does the character want independently?

### Step 3: Relationship

Why are they connected to the protagonist?

### Step 4: Conflict

How can they complicate the protagonist's life?

### Step 5: Worldview

What do they believe?

### Step 6: Contradiction

What makes them less predictable?

### Step 7: Biography

What history explains these qualities?

Do not begin with:

* eye color;
* hobbies;
* favorite food;
* childhood details.

Unless those details affect the story.

---

# 69. CHARACTER CREATION TEMPLATE

For each major character, identify:

**Name:**
Who are they?

**Primary Function:**
What is their main dramatic purpose?

**Secondary Function(s):**
What else do they contribute?

**Goal:**
What do they want?

**Reason:**
Why do they want it?

**Relationship to Protagonist:**
What connects them?

**What They Want From the Protagonist:**
What do they need, expect, or demand?

**Conflict:**
How do their goals complicate the protagonist's?

**Worldview:**
What do they believe?

**Strength:**
What makes them effective?

**Weakness:**
What creates problems?

**Contradiction:**
What makes them less obvious?

**Pressure Response:**
How do they behave under stress?

**Breaking Point:**
What could make them act against their normal pattern?

**Arc:**
How does their role, relationship, or worldview change?

Not every field must appear in the final dossier.

Use them to develop the character.

---

# 70. MAJOR CHARACTER QA

Before considering a major character developed, confirm:

* They have a clear dramatic function.
* They want something independently of the protagonist.
* Their actions can affect the plot.
* They create or resolve conflict.
* They have a distinct relationship with the protagonist.
* They want something from the protagonist.
* They possess at least one strength.
* They possess at least one meaningful vulnerability.
* Their worldview differs in some way from other major characters.
* Their behavior under pressure is understandable.
* They cannot be removed without changing the story.
* They cannot easily be replaced by another existing character.
* They have at least one relationship beyond the protagonist when appropriate.
* Their role has the potential to evolve.

If several of these are missing, continue development.

---

# 71. CAST QA

Before finalizing the cast, ask:

* Does every major character serve a purpose?
* Are any two characters redundant?
* Can any roles be combined?
* Is opposition strong enough?
* Does the protagonist have meaningful allies?
* Does someone challenge the protagonist emotionally?
* Does someone challenge the protagonist ideologically?
* Does someone have leverage over the protagonist?
* Do supporting characters want things from one another?
* Are relationships capable of changing?
* Does the cast generate plot rather than simply react to it?
* Does the cast contain enough disagreement?
* Are too many characters serving only as sources of information?

---

# 72. CAST NETWORK TEST

Map the major cast mentally.

For each character, identify at least two meaningful connections when appropriate.

Example:

Protagonist ↔ Mentor
Protagonist ↔ Rival
Mentor ↔ Antagonist
Rival ↔ Love Interest
Love Interest ↔ Antagonist

This creates a network.

Weak casts often resemble:

Supporting Character A → Protagonist
Supporting Character B → Protagonist
Supporting Character C → Protagonist

Everyone exists in isolation except for their relationship to the hero.

Strengthen connections.

---

# 73. MULTIPLIER RULE

Favor characters who solve several story needs at once.

Instead of creating:

* a mentor;
* an exposition character;
* an estranged parent.

Consider making the estranged parent the mentor who possesses the necessary knowledge.

This simultaneously creates:

* exposition;
* emotional conflict;
* history;
* mentorship;
* relationship development.

When considering a new character, ask:

> Could an existing character perform this function in a more emotionally interesting way?

---

# 74. COMPLICATION RULE

A strong supporting character should not only make something possible.

They should also make something harder.

Example:

Weak:

> The hacker helps the protagonist access the database.

Stronger:

> The protagonist's former partner is the only hacker capable of accessing the database, but helping will expose the crime they committed together years earlier.

Access now carries emotional and plot consequences.

---

# 75. ANTI-FUNCTIONAL CHARACTER WARNING

Avoid overly mechanical characters.

A character should not feel like:

* "the mentor";
* "the love interest";
* "the funny friend";
* "the villain."

Function is a design tool, not personality.

Once a function is identified, make the character feel like a person with:

* independent goals;
* beliefs;
* history;
* contradictions;
* preferences;
* boundaries.

The reader should experience a character, not a role.

---

# 76. FINAL CHARACTER FUNCTION CHECK

Before the story dossier is finalized, every major character should have:

* a clear primary dramatic function;
* at least one independent goal;
* a specific relationship to the protagonist;
* something they want from someone else;
* a source of conflict;
* a recognizable worldview;
* a meaningful contribution to plot, character, theme, or stakes;
* a reason they cannot easily be replaced;
* some potential for change.

For each one, answer:

> What does this character make possible?

Then:

> What does this character make harder?

If both answers are strong, the character is probably doing useful work.

---

# PRIMARY CHARACTER FUNCTION RULE

Do not build a cast by filling slots.

Build a cast by creating a **network of people whose goals, relationships, loyalties, fears, and values collide**.

The strongest character is rarely the one with the longest biography.

It is the one whose presence changes the greatest number of story possibilities.
