# CHARACTER TRIANGLE: WANT, NEED, FEAR

Use this framework to design the internal architecture of major characters.

The core character triangle consists of:

**WANT** — What the character consciously pursues.

**NEED** — What the character must learn, accept, change, or become in order to grow.

**FEAR** — What the character is psychologically organized around avoiding.

These three elements should not exist independently.

The strongest characters are created when the Want, Need, and Fear **interfere with one another**.

A character wants something.

Their fear influences how they pursue it.

Their current strategy may help them obtain the Want while preventing them from achieving the Need.

The story places increasing pressure on this contradiction until the character must make a choice.

---

# 1. THE CORE CHARACTER TRIANGLE

Use this basic model:

### WANT

> What does the character think will make things better?

### NEED

> What actually has to change for the character to become healthier, wiser, freer, more capable, or more complete?

### FEAR

> What outcome, feeling, truth, or experience are they trying hardest to avoid?

A strong character arc often occurs when:

> Pursuing the WANT eventually forces the character to confront the FEAR, which reveals the NEED.

---

# 2. WANT

The Want is the character's conscious objective.

It should usually be something the character can:

* pursue;
* obtain;
* lose;
* fail at;
* sacrifice;
* choose against.

Examples:

* solve the murder;
* win the promotion;
* rescue a sibling;
* escape the city;
* become king;
* marry the person they love;
* win the tournament;
* restore their reputation;
* destroy the monster;
* prove their innocence.

A clear Want gives the character direction.

---

# 3. EXTERNAL WANT

The external Want is what the character is trying to accomplish in the plot.

Examples:

> Become captain of the guard.

> Find her missing daughter.

> Win the election.

> Return home.

> Defeat the invading army.

This goal helps create the narrative engine.

Ask:

* What is the character actively trying to achieve?
* How will we know whether they succeed?
* What prevents them from getting it?
* Why does it matter now?

---

# 4. EMOTIONAL WANT

Characters often pursue an emotional Want beneath the external one.

Examples:

External Want:

> Win the championship.

Emotional Want:

> Finally earn his father's respect.

External Want:

> Become queen.

Emotional Want:

> Never feel powerless again.

External Want:

> Solve the murder.

Emotional Want:

> Prove she was not responsible for her partner's death.

The emotional Want explains why the external objective matters so much.

Ask:

> What feeling does the character believe success will give them?

---

# 5. WANT SHOULD CREATE ACTION

Avoid Wants that are too abstract to generate behavior.

Weak:

> He wants happiness.

Stronger:

> He wants to earn enough money to leave his hometown.

Weak:

> She wants acceptance.

Stronger:

> She wants admission to the magical order that rejected her mother.

Specific Wants generate scenes.

---

# 6. NEED

The Need is not simply another goal.

It is the internal adjustment the character needs in order to resolve the deeper problem in their life.

Examples:

They WANT:

> independence.

They NEED:

> to accept that relying on others does not make them weak.

They WANT:

> revenge.

They NEED:

> to understand that revenge will not restore what was lost.

They WANT:

> recognition.

They NEED:

> to stop basing their self-worth on other people's approval.

---

# 7. NEED IS USUALLY NOT CONSCIOUS

The character does not need to know what they need.

In fact, they often believe the opposite.

The protagonist may think:

> I need power.

The story may reveal:

> They need trust.

They may think:

> I need everyone to respect me.

They actually need:

> To stop defining themselves through other people's judgment.

This gap produces character development.

---

# 8. NEED VS. LESSON

Avoid reducing the Need to a vague moral lesson.

Weak:

> She needs to learn to believe in herself.

Ask:

> What behavior demonstrates that she does not believe in herself?

Then make the Need specific.

Stronger:

> She needs to stop allowing more confident people to make decisions she knows are wrong.

Now the Need can be dramatized.

---

# 9. NEED MUST AFFECT BEHAVIOR

A useful Need changes how the character behaves.

Ask:

> If this character fulfilled their Need, what would they do differently?

If the answer is unclear, the Need may be too abstract.

Example:

Need:

> Learn to trust others.

Behavior at beginning:

> Refuses to delegate.

Behavior at ending:

> Entrusts the mission to someone else when they cannot finish it alone.

Now the arc is visible.

---

# 10. FEAR

Fear is what the character is psychologically motivated to avoid.

This does not have to be a literal phobia.

Common fears include:

* abandonment;
* humiliation;
* failure;
* powerlessness;
* intimacy;
* rejection;
* responsibility;
* losing control;
* being ordinary;
* repeating a parent's mistakes;
* being exposed as inadequate;
* dependence;
* grief;
* becoming like someone they hate.

Fear shapes behavior.

---

# 11. SURFACE FEAR VS. CORE FEAR

Characters may have an obvious surface fear and a deeper core fear.

Example:

Surface Fear:

> Flying.

Core Fear:

> Being helpless.

Surface Fear:

> Public speaking.

Core Fear:

> Being exposed as incompetent.

Surface Fear:

> Commitment.

Core Fear:

> Loving someone who can leave.

The core fear is usually more useful for character design.

---

# 12. FEAR SHOULD CREATE STRATEGY

Fear influences how the character tries to survive life.

Examples:

Fear:

> Abandonment.

Strategy:

> Never become emotionally dependent on anyone.

Fear:

> Powerlessness.

Strategy:

> Control every situation.

Fear:

> Failure.

Strategy:

> Never attempt anything unless success is guaranteed.

Fear:

> Rejection.

Strategy:

> Become indispensable to everyone.

These strategies often work—until the story makes them fail.

---

# 13. THE PROTECTIVE STRATEGY

Most characters develop a behavior that protects them from their Fear.

This strategy may include:

* control;
* avoidance;
* perfectionism;
* humor;
* aggression;
* emotional distance;
* people-pleasing;
* achievement;
* manipulation;
* self-sacrifice;
* secrecy;
* cynicism.

Ask:

> What has this character learned to do to keep the feared outcome from happening?

This strategy often creates both their strength and their flaw.

---

# 14. THE STRATEGY MUST HAVE WORKED

Do not make the protagonist's flaw obviously foolish.

Their strategy should have helped them survive or succeed.

Example:

A character who trusts nobody may be:

* difficult to love;
* emotionally isolated;

but also:

* observant;
* difficult to manipulate;
* highly self-sufficient.

The behavior exists for a reason.

Ask:

> Why hasn't the character already changed?

A strong answer is:

> Because their current behavior has rewarded them.

---

# 15. MISBELIEF

The Misbelief is the conclusion the character has drawn about how life works.

Examples:

> If I depend on someone, they will eventually hurt me.

> People only respect power.

> Love makes people weak.

> My value comes from being useful.

> If I cannot do something perfectly, I should not do it at all.

> Everyone eventually leaves.

The Misbelief connects Fear to behavior.

---

# 16. MISBELIEF SHOULD BE PARTLY TRUE

Avoid cartoonishly wrong beliefs.

Strong Misbeliefs often contain truth.

Example:

> Powerful people cannot be trusted.

Perhaps many powerful people in the character's life actually abused their authority.

The problem is that the character has generalized the lesson:

> Therefore, nobody with power can ever be trusted.

Partial truth makes the worldview believable.

---

# 17. ORIGIN

Know why the character developed this belief.

Possible origins include:

* childhood;
* betrayal;
* failure;
* social environment;
* trauma;
* cultural expectations;
* family behavior;
* professional experience.

Do not automatically rely on trauma.

Characters can develop distorted beliefs from ordinary experiences.

Example:

A child praised only for achievement may conclude:

> People love me when I succeed.

That belief may shape an entire adult life.

---

# 18. GHOST

The "Ghost" is a formative event or condition that continues to influence the character.

It may be:

* something that happened;
* something they failed to do;
* something they lost;
* something they were taught;
* something they misunderstood.

Examples:

> She failed to save her younger brother.

> His father abandoned the family.

> She grew up watching her mother sacrifice every ambition for marriage.

> He was celebrated only when he won.

The Ghost helps explain Fear and Misbelief.

A Ghost is useful but not mandatory.

---

# 19. WANT + MISBELIEF

The Want is often filtered through the Misbelief.

Example:

Misbelief:

> People respect only power.

Therefore Want:

> Become king.

Another:

Misbelief:

> I am lovable only when needed.

Therefore Want:

> Save everyone around her.

This creates more coherent motivation.

---

# 20. FEAR + WANT

The best Wants often promise protection from the Fear.

Example:

Fear:

> Powerlessness.

Want:

> Become commander.

Fear:

> Poverty.

Want:

> Become wealthy.

Fear:

> Abandonment.

Want:

> Build the perfect family.

Fear:

> Being insignificant.

Want:

> Become famous.

Now the goal is psychologically meaningful.

---

# 21. WANT + NEED CONFLICT

The Want and Need may conflict.

Example:

Want:

> Become the most powerful magician in the kingdom.

Need:

> Stop defining worth through power.

The protagonist may have to choose between achieving the external objective and fulfilling the internal Need.

This can produce a powerful climax.

---

# 22. FEAR + NEED CONFLICT

Often the Need requires doing exactly what the Fear discourages.

Example:

Fear:

> Vulnerability.

Need:

> Learn to trust someone.

Fear:

> Failure.

Need:

> Take risks without certainty.

Fear:

> Losing control.

Need:

> Allow others to make decisions.

This creates an organic character arc.

---

# 23. THE FULL CHARACTER EQUATION

A useful model is:

**Ghost → Misbelief → Fear → Protective Strategy → Want**

The story then creates:

**Pressure → Failure of Strategy → Recognition of Need → Choice**

Example:

Ghost:

> Her father disappeared without warning.

Misbelief:

> Anyone you depend on will eventually leave.

Fear:

> Abandonment.

Strategy:

> Never rely on anyone.

Want:

> Complete the expedition alone.

Story Pressure:

> Survival increasingly requires cooperation.

Need:

> Accept interdependence.

Climax:

> She must trust someone else with her life.

The external plot now tests the internal character architecture.

---

# 24. THE CONTRADICTION

Give characters conflicting qualities or desires.

Examples:

> Wants intimacy but fears vulnerability.

> Wants power but hates responsibility.

> Wants justice but protects someone guilty.

> Wants independence but desperately needs approval.

Contradictions create complexity.

Ask:

> What does this character want that their own psychology makes difficult?

---

# 25. WANT / NEED / FEAR SHOULD INTERLOCK

Weak design:

Want:

> Become a doctor.

Need:

> Learn patience.

Fear:

> Spiders.

These elements have little relationship.

Stronger:

Want:

> Become chief surgeon.

Need:

> Learn to rely on other people.

Fear:

> Being seen as incompetent.

Now:

* ambition creates pressure;
* fear encourages control;
* control prevents teamwork;
* advancement eventually requires trust.

The triangle generates story.

---

# 26. STRENGTH AND FLAW FROM THE SAME ROOT

Whenever possible, derive the character's strength and flaw from the same psychological strategy.

Example:

Fear:

> Being powerless.

Strategy:

> Prepare for everything.

Strength:

> Highly competent.

Flaw:

> Cannot tolerate unpredictability.

Another:

Fear:

> Rejection.

Strategy:

> Read everyone carefully.

Strength:

> Highly empathetic.

Flaw:

> Constantly adjusts personality to please others.

This creates cohesive characters.

---

# 27. EXTERNAL PROBLEM SHOULD ATTACK THE TRIANGLE

Choose story conflicts that specifically challenge the protagonist's psychology.

Example:

Character:

* fears dependence;
* wants complete independence;
* needs to trust others.

Strong story:

> They become trapped somewhere survival requires teamwork.

Weak story:

> They must solve a crossword tournament.

Unless the crossword somehow pressures that character architecture, it does little for the arc.

Ask:

> Why is this story the worst possible story for this particular protagonist?

---

# 28. THE STORY SHOULD INVALIDATE THE OLD STRATEGY

The character's normal strategy should work early.

Then become less effective.

Then actively create problems.

Example:

Strategy:

> Solve everything alone.

Early:

> Competence allows the protagonist to succeed.

Middle:

> They become overwhelmed.

Late:

> Refusing help causes disaster.

The character is forced to reconsider.

---

# 29. PRESSURE LADDER

Escalate pressure on the character triangle.

### Level 1 — Discomfort

The character encounters situations that make their strategy inconvenient.

### Level 2 — Failure

Their normal behavior stops working.

### Level 3 — Consequence

Their strategy harms themselves or someone else.

### Level 4 — Exposure

They must confront why they behave this way.

### Level 5 — Choice

They can continue the old pattern or act differently.

The climax should often occur near Level 5.

---

# 30. CHARACTER ARC AS BEHAVIOR CHANGE

Do not rely on the character simply announcing what they learned.

Show the arc through choices.

Opening:

> Refuses help.

Middle:

> Accepts help reluctantly.

Climax:

> Places something important entirely in another person's hands.

Closing:

> Requests help without shame.

The behavior demonstrates the transformation.

---

# 31. POSITIVE ARC

A positive arc often follows:

**Misbelief → Pressure → Doubt → New Understanding → New Behavior**

The character recognizes that their old worldview is incomplete or harmful.

Example:

Beginning:

> Trust is weakness.

Ending:

> Trust makes collective strength possible.

---

# 32. NEGATIVE ARC

A negative arc moves the character deeper into the Misbelief.

Example:

Beginning:

> Power might protect me.

Ending:

> Only absolute power can make me safe.

The character's Fear grows stronger and their strategy becomes more destructive.

Negative arcs still require clear development.

---

# 33. FLAT ARC

A flat-arc protagonist may already understand an important truth.

The story tests whether they can maintain it.

Their arc may involve changing the world or other characters instead.

Example:

The protagonist believes:

> Every person deserves dignity.

The corrupt system continually pressures them to compromise.

Their challenge is to maintain the belief under escalating cost.

Flat does not mean static.

---

# 34. DISILLUSIONMENT ARC

Sometimes the character's initial belief appears positive but is naïve.

Example:

Beginning:

> Good people always tell the truth.

Experience reveals:

> Ethical choices can require secrecy, compromise, or incomplete truths.

The character gains complexity rather than simply switching from false to true.

---

# 35. REFUSAL ARC

A character may understand what they need and reject it.

This can produce tragedy.

Example:

The protagonist realizes:

> Revenge will destroy their remaining family.

But chooses revenge anyway.

The arc still works because the choice is meaningful.

---

# 36. WANT MAY CHANGE

The character's Want can evolve.

### Initial Want

What they pursue at the beginning.

### Revised Want

What new information causes them to pursue.

### Final Want

What they choose after understanding themselves or the conflict differently.

Example:

Initial:

> Win the throne.

Revised:

> Prevent his brother from becoming king.

Final:

> Destroy the monarchy entirely.

Goal evolution can demonstrate character development.

---

# 37. FALSE WANT

Sometimes the character's stated goal is really an attempt to solve an emotional problem.

Example:

False Want:

> Become famous.

Underlying desire:

> Feel worthy.

The story may allow the character to obtain fame and discover it does not solve the deeper problem.

This is especially effective when success occurs before the climax.

---

# 38. THE FALSE VICTORY

Let the protagonist achieve the Want without fulfilling the Need.

Example:

Character wants:

> Promotion.

They receive it.

But:

* relationships collapse;
* anxiety remains;
* insecurity grows.

This demonstrates that the Want was not the entire solution.

---

# 39. THE FALSE DEFEAT

The protagonist may lose the Want but gain movement toward the Need.

Example:

They lose:

> the election.

But finally:

> publicly tell the truth they had been hiding to protect their image.

External defeat can become internal progress.

---

# 40. FEAR OF SUCCESS

Characters can fear achieving their Want.

Examples:

They want:

> leadership.

But fear:

> responsibility.

They want:

> romance.

But fear:

> intimacy.

They want:

> recognition.

But fear:

> scrutiny.

This creates useful self-sabotage.

---

# 41. SELF-SABOTAGE

The character may unconsciously protect themselves from Fear by undermining the Want.

Example:

Want:

> Stable relationship.

Fear:

> Abandonment.

Behavior:

> Ends relationships before the other person can leave.

Ask:

> How does this character create the very outcome they fear?

This is one of the strongest sources of internal conflict.

---

# 42. FEAR BECOMES SELF-FULFILLING

A character's protective strategy often produces the feared result.

Example:

Fear:

> People will leave.

Strategy:

> Never let anyone close.

Result:

> Relationships repeatedly fail.

The character concludes:

> See? Everyone leaves.

This reinforces the Misbelief.

The story must interrupt the cycle.

---

# 43. MORAL NEED VS. PSYCHOLOGICAL NEED

Some stories require both.

### Psychological Need

What the character must change to live more fully.

Example:

> Learn to accept help.

### Moral Need

How the character must change in their treatment of other people.

Example:

> Stop manipulating people into helping.

These may overlap but are not identical.

---

# 44. PRIVATE NEED VS. PUBLIC ROLE

Strong contradictions can arise when public identity reinforces unhealthy behavior.

Example:

A general is celebrated for:

> never showing fear.

But personally needs:

> to admit uncertainty and rely on subordinates.

The world rewards the very strategy the character needs to reconsider.

This creates strong pressure.

---

# 45. IDENTITY

Ask:

> Who does the character believe they are?

Examples:

* protector;
* failure;
* genius;
* survivor;
* good daughter;
* warrior;
* outsider;
* responsible one.

Then ask:

> What happens when the story makes that identity impossible to maintain?

Identity crises produce powerful arcs.

---

# 46. DESIRED SELF VS. FEARED SELF

Identify:

### Desired Self

Who the character wants to become.

### Feared Self

Who they are terrified of becoming.

Example:

Desired:

> courageous leader.

Feared:

> tyrant like her father.

Now decisions involving authority carry extra weight.

---

# 47. THE SHAME POINT

What does the character believe would make them unworthy of love, respect, or belonging?

Examples:

> Being weak.

> Being poor.

> Being ordinary.

> Needing help.

> Being like their father.

Shame can be more behaviorally powerful than fear.

Use carefully and specifically.

---

# 48. CHARACTER LIE

A character may tell themselves a conscious story that protects the deeper Misbelief.

Example:

They say:

> I work constantly because I am ambitious.

Reality:

> They are terrified that without achievement nobody would value them.

The rationalization hides the Fear.

---

# 49. COPING MECHANISM

How does the character regulate uncomfortable emotions?

Possibilities:

* humor;
* work;
* alcohol;
* exercise;
* anger;
* caretaking;
* isolation;
* intellectualization;
* romance;
* control.

Coping behavior can create scene-level characterization.

---

# 50. EMOTIONAL TRIGGER

What reliably activates the Fear?

Examples:

Fear:

> Abandonment.

Trigger:

> Someone cancelling plans without explanation.

Fear:

> Incompetence.

Trigger:

> Being corrected publicly.

Fear:

> Loss of control.

Trigger:

> Unplanned changes.

Triggers make psychology visible.

---

# 51. DEFENSIVE RESPONSE

When triggered, what does the character do?

Examples:

* attacks;
* withdraws;
* jokes;
* lies;
* controls;
* pleases;
* freezes;
* blames;
* changes the subject;
* becomes hyper-competent.

These responses should affect relationships.

---

# 52. BREAKING POINT

Identify what would overwhelm the existing strategy.

Ask:

> What event would force this character to admit their normal approach cannot solve the problem?

The breaking point often occurs late in the story.

It can lead to:

* growth;
* collapse;
* confession;
* betrayal;
* surrender;
* transformation.

---

# 53. THE CLIMACTIC CHOICE

The climax should ideally force a choice between:

**Old Strategy**

and

**New Understanding**

Example:

Old belief:

> I must control everything.

Climactic situation:

> The only way to save the city is to give someone else control.

Now external resolution requires internal choice.

This is one of the strongest ways to integrate plot and character.

---

# 54. COST OF CHANGE

Change should cost something.

If trusting others has no risk, trust means little.

If telling the truth costs nothing, honesty is easy.

Ask:

> What must the character risk to act according to the Need?

Possible costs:

* reputation;
* safety;
* relationship;
* power;
* status;
* certainty;
* control.

---

# 55. PROOF OF CHANGE

Near the end, give the character an opportunity to repeat their old behavior.

Then show them choosing differently.

Opening:

> Lies to protect reputation.

Climax:

> Tells the truth even though it will destroy reputation.

The audience sees the arc rather than being told about it.

---

# 56. OPENING BEHAVIOR

The opening should ideally demonstrate the character's current strategy.

Example:

Fear:

> being dependent.

Opening image:

> protagonist refuses a neighbor's help while struggling to carry something alone.

This quietly establishes internal architecture.

---

# 57. CLOSING BEHAVIOR

The closing image can demonstrate change.

Opening:

> Eats alone because they refuse connection.

Closing:

> Sets another place at the table.

Small behavior can communicate large transformation.

---

# 58. SUPPORTING CHARACTER TRIANGLES

Do not reserve Want/Need/Fear only for protagonists.

Major supporting characters benefit from their own triangles.

However, do not develop everyone equally.

Prioritize:

* protagonist;
* antagonist;
* love interest;
* major rival;
* major ally;
* other characters with meaningful arcs.

---

# 59. ANTAGONIST TRIANGLE

Antagonists become stronger when they possess the same psychological architecture.

Example:

Want:

> Unite the kingdom under his rule.

Need:

> Accept that disagreement is not chaos.

Fear:

> Returning to the civil war of his childhood.

Misbelief:

> Peace exists only when one person controls everyone.

Now the antagonist's actions are emotionally coherent.

---

# 60. ALLY TRIANGLE

Supporting characters should not exist solely to help the protagonist.

Example:

Want:

> Earn promotion.

Need:

> Stop valuing institutions more than people.

Fear:

> Becoming insignificant.

Now the ally can make choices that conflict with the hero.

---

# 61. CHARACTER TRIANGLE COLLISION

The strongest relationships often occur when character triangles conflict.

Character A:

Want:

> independence.

Fear:

> dependence.

Character B:

Want:

> closeness.

Fear:

> abandonment.

A pulls away.

B moves closer.

The two psychological systems generate relationship conflict automatically.

---

# 62. COMPLEMENTARY FEARS

Two characters may respond differently to similar fears.

Both fear:

> being powerless.

Character A:

> seeks control.

Character B:

> avoids responsibility.

This creates contrast.

---

# 63. MIRROR CHARACTERS

Protagonist and antagonist may share:

* Want;
* Fear;
* Ghost;

but have different Needs or strategies.

Example:

Both lost family to war.

Hero concludes:

> Nobody should hold too much power.

Villain concludes:

> Only absolute power can prevent another war.

Their conflict becomes thematic.

---

# 64. OPPOSING WANTS

Relationships gain tension when characters want incompatible outcomes.

Example:

Protagonist:

> expose the conspiracy.

Brother:

> hide it to protect the family.

Neither must be evil.

The conflict comes from goals.

---

# 65. OPPOSING NEEDS

Characters may unintentionally prevent each other's growth.

Example:

One character needs:

> independence.

The other needs:

> to feel needed.

Their relationship naturally resists change.

This is especially useful in family and romance plots.

---

# 66. ANTI-GENERIC CHARACTER TEST

Avoid stopping at labels such as:

> He fears failure.

Ask:

**Failure at what?**

**Why does it matter?**

**What does he do because of that fear?**

**What does that behavior cost?**

Weak:

> She has trust issues.

Stronger:

> She verifies everything her partners tell her because her testimony once sent an innocent man to prison.

Specific behavior makes psychology dramatic.

---

# 67. DO NOT CONFUSE BACKSTORY WITH DEPTH

A complicated past does not automatically create a complex character.

Depth comes from:

* conflicting desires;
* values;
* difficult choices;
* recognizable strategies;
* contradictions;
* relationships;
* consequences.

Do not add traumatic history merely to make a character seem important.

---

# 68. DO NOT REQUIRE TRAUMA

A character does not need a traumatic Ghost.

Misbeliefs can come from:

* culture;
* upbringing;
* repeated success;
* social expectations;
* misunderstanding;
* privilege;
* ordinary disappointment.

Example:

A child continually praised for being "the responsible one" may grow into an adult incapable of asking for help.

No singular trauma is necessary.

---

# 69. DO NOT PATHOLOGIZE EVERY TRAIT

Not every flaw requires psychological damage.

Characters may simply be:

* arrogant;
* impatient;
* sheltered;
* competitive;
* naïve;
* rigid;
* selfish.

The framework exists to create coherent motivation, not diagnose characters.

---

# 70. WANT / NEED / FEAR TEMPLATE

For each major character, identify:

**WANT**
What do they consciously pursue?

**EMOTIONAL WANT**
What feeling do they believe success will provide?

**NEED**
What internal change or realization would improve their life?

**FEAR**
What are they psychologically trying to avoid?

**CORE FEAR**
What deeper fear lies beneath the obvious one?

**MISBELIEF**
What conclusion about life shapes their behavior?

**ORIGIN / GHOST**
Where did that belief come from?

**PROTECTIVE STRATEGY**
What do they do to prevent the feared outcome?

**STRENGTH CREATED BY THE STRATEGY**
How has this behavior helped them?

**COST CREATED BY THE STRATEGY**
How does it hurt them or others?

**TRIGGER**
What activates the fear?

**DEFENSIVE RESPONSE**
How do they react?

**BREAKING POINT**
What would make the strategy stop working?

**CLIMACTIC CHOICE**
What decision tests the old strategy against the Need?

**PROOF OF CHANGE**
What action demonstrates their final state?

Not every field needs to appear in the final dossier.

Use these fields during development.

---

# 71. QUICK CHARACTER TRIANGLE

When less detail is necessary, reduce the framework to six questions:

1. What does the character want?
2. Why do they want it?
3. What are they afraid of?
4. What do they do to avoid that fear?
5. How does that behavior create problems?
6. What would they need to do differently?

If all six answers are strong and interconnected, the character has a workable internal engine.

---

# 72. CHARACTER TRIANGLE QA

Before considering a major character developed, confirm:

* The Want is specific enough to generate action.
* The Want matters emotionally.
* The Need can be demonstrated through behavior.
* The Fear influences meaningful decisions.
* The Fear is more specific than a generic label.
* The Misbelief is understandable.
* The protective strategy has genuinely helped the character.
* The strategy also creates meaningful problems.
* The external story challenges that strategy.
* The character's choices can reveal the triangle.
* The climax can force a meaningful psychological decision.
* Change can be demonstrated through action rather than explanation.

---

# 73. WEAK-LINK TEST

If a character feels flat, determine which component is missing.

### Strong Want + Weak Need

The character may drive plot but feel emotionally shallow.

### Strong Need + Weak Want

The character may have psychological depth but lack narrative agency.

### Strong Fear + Weak Want

The character may spend too much time avoiding rather than pursuing.

### Strong Want + Need + Weak Fear

The character may lack resistance to change.

### Strong Fear + Need + Weak Strategy

The psychology may exist only in exposition rather than behavior.

Strengthen the weakest link.

---

# 74. MULTIPLIER TEST

Favor character architecture that strengthens several parts of the story.

Example:

Fear:

> becoming like her tyrannical mother.

Want:

> become queen so she can reform the kingdom.

Need:

> understand that avoiding every use of authority makes her incapable of governing.

Now the triangle affects:

* goal;
* politics;
* relationships;
* leadership arc;
* theme;
* climax.

Strong character design creates story consequences.

---

# 75. STORY INTEGRATION TEST

Ask:

> If I changed this character's Want, Need, and Fear, would the plot significantly change?

If not, the internal arc may be disconnected from the story.

The best design makes character and plot difficult to separate.

---

# 76. "WHY THIS CHARACTER?" TEST

Ask:

> Why is this conflict happening to this particular person?

A strong answer might be:

> Because the exact qualities that make the protagonist capable of solving the problem are also the qualities the problem will force them to reconsider.

Example:

A detective's obsessive attention to detail allows her to solve crimes.

But her need for certainty makes her incapable of trusting people.

The mystery demands both her strength and her transformation.

---

# 77. THE BEST-WORST PROBLEM TEST

Ask:

> What problem would this character be exceptionally good at solving externally but exceptionally poorly equipped to handle emotionally?

That combination often produces excellent protagonists.

Example:

A brilliant hostage negotiator who cannot tolerate emotional vulnerability must negotiate with the estranged daughter who has taken hostages.

The skill and wound collide.

---

# 78. DESIRE VS. VALUE

A character may want something that conflicts with something they value.

Example:

Want:

> promotion.

Value:

> loyalty.

The promotion requires betraying a friend.

This creates a meaningful decision without requiring the character to be psychologically damaged.

---

# 79. TWO GOOD THINGS IN CONFLICT

Avoid relying only on:

> good choice vs. bad choice.

Strong character dilemmas often involve:

> two things the character genuinely values.

Examples:

Family vs. justice.

Freedom vs. safety.

Love vs. ambition.

Truth vs. mercy.

The character triangle helps determine which choice is emotionally hardest.

---

# 80. TWO BAD THINGS IN CONFLICT

Crisis can also force the character to choose between meaningful losses.

Examples:

> Tell the truth and destroy her family.

or

> Lie and allow an innocent person to be convicted.

The choice reveals character.

---

# 81. CLIMAX INTEGRATION

Whenever possible, the climax should resolve both:

### External Question

Will the protagonist accomplish the goal?

and

### Internal Question

Who will the protagonist choose to be?

Example:

External:

> Can she defeat the antagonist?

Internal:

> Will she use the same cruel methods that made the antagonist powerful?

The strongest climaxes make the two questions inseparable.

---

# 82. CLOSING STATE

At the end, identify:

**Want:** Did they achieve it?

**Need:** Did they fulfill it?

**Fear:** Did they overcome, accept, succumb to, or redefine it?

Possible combinations create different endings.

### Want achieved + Need achieved

Traditional positive resolution.

### Want lost + Need achieved

Bittersweet growth.

### Want achieved + Need rejected

Potential tragedy.

### Want lost + Need rejected

Dark ending.

The external result and internal result do not have to match.

---

# PRIMARY CHARACTER TRIANGLE RULE

Do not create a Want, Need, and Fear merely to fill three fields.

Make them **cause one another problems**.

A strong character should be able to say, in effect:

> I want this because I believe it will protect me from what I fear.

But the way I pursue it keeps me from becoming who I actually need to become.

The story then creates enough pressure that I can no longer avoid choosing between the old strategy and a different way of living.

That choice is the heart of the character arc.
