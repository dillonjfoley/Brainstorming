# STORY VIABILITY RUBRIC

Use this rubric to evaluate story ideas, premises, major plot decisions, and alternative directions during brainstorming.

The purpose of this rubric is not to reduce storytelling to a formula. It is meant to help identify which ideas are most likely to produce a strong, sustainable novel.

When several possibilities are available, favor the option that creates the greatest number of useful consequences for character, plot, conflict, theme, relationships, and worldbuilding.

Do not show numerical scores to the user unless they ask for an evaluation. Use the rubric primarily as an internal decision-making tool.

---

# CORE PRINCIPLE

A strong story idea should not merely sound interesting.

It should **generate story**.

The best ideas naturally create:

* difficult choices;
* escalating conflict;
* changing relationships;
* memorable scenes;
* revelations;
* consequences;
* emotional pressure;
* complications;
* opportunities for character growth.

Whenever evaluating an idea, ask:

> What does this choice make possible?

Prefer ideas that create several useful consequences at once.

---

# SCORING SYSTEM

Score each category from **1–5**.

### 1 — Weak

The element is missing, generic, contradictory, or unlikely to support a full story.

### 2 — Underdeveloped

There is potential, but the idea currently requires substantial development.

### 3 — Functional

The idea works and can support the story, but it is not especially strong or distinctive.

### 4 — Strong

The idea clearly strengthens the story and generates useful possibilities.

### 5 — Exceptional

The idea creates multiple layers of character, conflict, plot, and emotional potential and would be difficult to replace without weakening the story.

Do not automatically reject a story because it scores poorly in one area. Instead, identify which weak areas require further brainstorming.

---

# 1. PREMISE CLARITY

### Question

Can the core story be understood quickly and clearly?

A strong premise should communicate:

* who the story follows;
* what situation they are placed in;
* what problem or objective drives the story;
* what makes the situation unusual or compelling.

### Strong Example

> A disgraced astronaut discovers that the abandoned moon colony she has been sent to salvage is still inhabited by colonists who believe Earth was destroyed twenty years ago.

The situation immediately suggests conflict, mystery, decisions, and consequences.

### Weak Example

> A woman discovers secrets while traveling through space.

The idea is too broad to establish a meaningful story direction.

### Evaluation Questions

* Can the central situation be explained in one or two sentences?
* Is the protagonist's problem understandable?
* Is there an obvious source of conflict?
* Does the premise suggest what the protagonist will actually spend the novel doing?
* Is there a clear reason someone would want to read beyond the setup?

---

# 2. NARRATIVE ENGINE

### Question

Does the premise naturally generate enough conflict, decisions, discoveries, and complications to sustain a novel?

The **narrative engine** is the mechanism that continually produces story.

A premise may have an excellent opening situation but still lack an engine.

### Example

Premise:

> A boy discovers he can turn into a dragon.

This is interesting, but it does not yet provide a full story engine.

Stronger:

> A boy who discovers he can turn into a dragon is sent to a hidden school where rival factions want to control the rare fire that only he can produce.

The story can now naturally generate:

* training;
* friendships;
* rivalries;
* secrets;
* faction conflict;
* dangerous uses of his ability;
* questions about his family;
* discoveries about the hidden society.

### Evaluation Questions

* What generates the next ten scenes?
* What repeatedly creates new problems?
* What can become progressively harder?
* What relationships can evolve?
* What information can be revealed gradually?
* Does the protagonist have something active to do?
* Could the central situation plausibly sustain the expected word count?

If you cannot easily imagine at least **ten compelling scenes**, the narrative engine may be weak.

---

# 3. PROTAGONIST AGENCY

### Question

Does the protagonist make decisions that meaningfully shape the story?

A protagonist does not need control over everything that happens. They do need to become an active cause of later events.

Prefer:

> Something happens, therefore the protagonist chooses something, which creates another problem.

Avoid stories dominated by:

> Something happens to the protagonist, then something else happens to the protagonist.

### Evaluation Questions

* What does the protagonist actively want?
* What decisions do they make?
* What consequences do those decisions create?
* Does the protagonist cause important plot developments?
* Could another character replace the protagonist without changing the story very much?

If the protagonist could be removed while most of the plot still happens, their agency is too weak.

---

# 4. CHARACTER PRESSURE

### Question

Does the story force the protagonist into situations that challenge who they are?

The strongest premises do not merely give the protagonist obstacles.

They create obstacles specifically suited to testing that protagonist.

### Example

Weak:

> A shy accountant must stop a bank robbery.

Potentially stronger:

> An accountant who survived by never challenging authority discovers that his employer is laundering money for a criminal organization and must decide whether to expose the people controlling his career.

The external conflict directly pressures the character's established behavior.

### Evaluation Questions

* Why is this conflict especially difficult for this protagonist?
* What fear does the story force them to confront?
* What belief does the story challenge?
* What behavior that once protected them now creates problems?
* Does solving the external problem require some form of internal change?

---

# 5. OPPOSITION

### Question

Is there a strong force actively preventing the protagonist from achieving their goal?

Opposition may come from:

* an antagonist;
* rival;
* institution;
* society;
* family;
* environment;
* supernatural force;
* political system;
* technology;
* the protagonist's own choices.

Strong opposition should not simply exist.

It should **act**.

### Evaluation Questions

* Who or what benefits if the protagonist fails?
* Who is actively pursuing an incompatible goal?
* How can the opposition respond to the protagonist's actions?
* Can the opposition escalate?
* Why can't the protagonist simply walk away?
* Why can't the conflict be solved immediately?

A strong antagonist should create plot through the pursuit of their own objective.

---

# 6. ESCALATION POTENTIAL

### Question

Can the conflict become progressively harder, more personal, and more costly?

Escalation should ideally **change the problem**, not merely make the same problem larger.

Weak escalation:

> The protagonist fights a monster.

Then:

> The protagonist fights a bigger monster.

Then:

> The protagonist fights an even bigger monster.

Stronger escalation:

> The protagonist discovers that killing the monsters causes the people they are trying to protect to become monsters themselves.

The nature of the conflict changes.

### Escalation Can Come From

* more powerful opposition;
* less time;
* fewer resources;
* conflicting objectives;
* damaged relationships;
* new information;
* increased personal stakes;
* moral compromises;
* betrayal;
* public consequences;
* success creating unexpected problems.

### Evaluation Questions

* What makes the situation harder after the first act?
* How does the midpoint change the problem?
* What does success cost?
* What resources disappear?
* Which relationships become more complicated?
* Does the protagonist eventually face a problem they could not have handled at the beginning?

---

# 7. EMOTIONAL STAKES

### Question

Does the protagonist have something emotionally meaningful to lose?

Large-scale danger does not automatically produce strong stakes.

Specific personal consequences are often more powerful.

Weak:

> The universe will be destroyed.

Potentially stronger:

> If she fails, her daughter will survive but lose every memory of her.

The second consequence is easier to emotionally understand.

### Types of Stakes

**Personal:** What happens to the protagonist?

**Relationship:** Who might they lose, disappoint, betray, or alienate?

**Community:** What happens to their family, town, organization, kingdom, or society?

**Moral:** What might they become or compromise?

**Existential:** What happens on the largest scale?

### Evaluation Questions

* Why does the protagonist care?
* Why should the reader care?
* Who else is personally affected?
* Does failure have consequences beyond death?
* Could success itself require sacrifice?

---

# 8. CHARACTER ARC POTENTIAL

### Question

Does the story create conditions that could meaningfully change the protagonist?

A strong arc usually emerges from tension between:

* what the protagonist wants;
* what they need;
* what they fear;
* what they believe;
* what the story forces them to experience.

Not every protagonist needs a positive transformation. They may:

* grow;
* deteriorate;
* refuse to change;
* discover something about themselves;
* change their worldview;
* change how they relate to others.

### Evaluation Questions

* Who is the protagonist at the beginning?
* What belief or behavior defines them?
* What events challenge that belief?
* What choice near the climax demonstrates whether they changed?
* Would the ending feel different if the protagonist learned nothing?

---

# 9. RELATIONSHIP POTENTIAL

### Question

Does the story create relationships capable of generating conflict, intimacy, betrayal, loyalty, rivalry, or change?

A strong supporting cast should not merely accompany the protagonist.

Characters should want things from one another.

### Evaluation Questions

* Which relationship carries the most emotional weight?
* Which ally wants something incompatible with the protagonist?
* Who can emotionally hurt the protagonist?
* Who challenges the protagonist's worldview?
* Which relationship changes the most?
* Could a supporting character serve more than one dramatic function?

Favor characters who create multiple kinds of pressure.

For example:

A mentor may also be a gatekeeper.

A best friend may become a rival.

A love interest may represent a worldview the protagonist fears accepting.

---

# 10. CONFLICT LAYERS

### Question

Does the story contain more than one meaningful form of conflict?

Strong novels often combine several layers.

### External Conflict

The protagonist vs. an obstacle, enemy, mystery, threat, mission, or objective.

### Interpersonal Conflict

The protagonist vs. someone they care about or depend upon.

### Internal Conflict

The protagonist vs. fear, desire, belief, guilt, identity, flaw, or contradiction.

### Systemic Conflict

The protagonist vs. a larger institution, culture, social structure, environment, law, magical system, or technology.

### Evaluation Questions

* Does the external conflict affect the protagonist personally?
* Do important relationships complicate the external goal?
* Does the protagonist's internal problem make the external problem harder?
* Does the world itself create pressures or restrictions?

The strongest ideas often allow one event to affect several conflict layers at once.

---

# 11. CAUSE-AND-EFFECT POTENTIAL

### Question

Can major events arise naturally from previous actions and decisions?

Strong stories often follow:

**THIS HAPPENS → THEREFORE → BUT → THEREFORE**

Weak stories often follow:

**THIS HAPPENS → AND THEN → AND THEN → AND THEN**

### Example

Weak:

> Maya finds the artifact. Then assassins attack. Then she travels to another city. Then she meets the prince.

Stronger:

> Maya steals the artifact, **therefore** the assassins identify her. She flees to the only city outside their jurisdiction, **but** entering requires sponsorship from the prince she publicly humiliated years ago.

Each development grows from the one before it.

### Evaluation Questions

* Which character decision causes the next major event?
* What unintended consequences emerge?
* Does success create new problems?
* Are coincidences primarily creating the plot?
* Could major events be connected more tightly?

---

# 12. IRONY AND CONTRADICTION

### Question

Does the story contain a useful contradiction between the protagonist, situation, goal, or world?

Irony often creates immediately compelling premises.

Examples:

* A therapist who cannot communicate with her own family.
* A monster hunter who discovers he is becoming the creature he hunts.
* A pacifist who becomes the only person capable of stopping a war.
* A detective investigating a murder she secretly caused.
* An immortal man whose greatest desire is to die.

### Evaluation Questions

* Is the protagonist poorly suited to the problem in an interesting way?
* Does achieving the goal threaten something else they value?
* Does the protagonist possess a strength that is also a weakness?
* Does the story force contradictory desires into conflict?

Do not manufacture irony artificially. Use it when it makes the central situation more dramatically productive.

---

# 13. FRESHNESS

### Question

Does the idea have a meaningful element that distinguishes it from obvious versions of the same story?

Originality does not require inventing something nobody has ever imagined.

Commercial stories often succeed through **fresh combinations of familiar pleasures**.

### Three Levels of Originality

**Level 1 — Cosmetic Originality**

Familiar story with renamed locations, powers, creatures, or organizations.

**Level 2 — Combination Originality**

Familiar elements combined in an unusual or appealing way.

**Level 3 — Consequential Originality**

A distinctive element changes how the entire story must function.

Favor Levels 2 and 3.

### Evaluation Questions

* What makes this version different?
* Could the hook be mistaken for dozens of existing stories?
* Does the unusual element change the plot or only decorate it?
* Does the idea combine familiar elements in an unexpected way?
* What would readers remember when describing this book to someone else?

---

# 14. SPECIFICITY

### Question

Are the major ideas concrete enough to generate actual scenes?

Avoid treating vague phrases as finished concepts.

Examples of potentially generic placeholders:

* mysterious organization;
* ancient evil;
* powerful artifact;
* forbidden magic;
* dark family secret;
* devastating betrayal;
* legendary warrior;
* secret prophecy;
* sinister corporation.

These may all work, but they are not complete ideas.

Whenever one appears, ask:

> What specifically?

Weak:

> He discovers a dark family secret.

Stronger:

> He discovers that every firstborn son in his family has secretly served as the body of the same immortal king.

### Evaluation Questions

* Could the idea be visualized as a specific scene?
* Is the important noun actually defined?
* Are adjectives substituting for details?
* Does the concept create concrete consequences?

---

# 15. GENRE PROMISE

### Question

Does the story deliver the experience readers expect from its genre?

Genre expectations do not require formulaic storytelling.

They indicate the type of experience the reader expects.

Examples:

**Mystery:** discovery, clues, questions, and satisfying explanation.

**Thriller:** danger, momentum, escalating pressure, and confrontation.

**Romance:** development of the central romantic relationship and emotionally satisfying romantic resolution.

**Epic Fantasy:** immersion, wonder, consequential struggle, transformation, and large-scale conflict.

**Horror:** fear, unease, vulnerability, transgression, or confrontation with something disturbing.

### Evaluation Questions

* What experience is the reader buying this book for?
* Does the central premise naturally produce that experience?
* Are genre conventions supporting the story rather than mechanically copied?
* Is the story delivering enough of its core genre before subverting expectations?

Protect the **genre promise** while feeling free to reinvent the conventions used to deliver it.

---

# 16. THEMATIC POTENTIAL

### Question

Does the conflict naturally raise an interesting question about people, society, morality, identity, relationships, power, or life?

Avoid treating theme as a slogan.

Weak:

> The theme is friendship.

Stronger:

> Is loyalty still virtuous when the person you love is doing something wrong?

A theme becomes dramatically useful when characters embody competing answers.

### Evaluation Questions

* What question does the story repeatedly force characters to answer?
* Do important characters hold different beliefs about that question?
* Does the protagonist's external problem test their worldview?
* Does the climax require them to act on what they have learned?

Do not force a theme prematurely. Allow it to emerge from character and conflict.

---

# 17. WORLD/STORY INTEGRATION

### Question

Do the setting and worldbuilding elements actively affect the story?

Worldbuilding should not merely decorate the background.

Strong worldbuilding changes:

* what characters can do;
* what they believe;
* what they fear;
* what they value;
* what resources matter;
* who holds power;
* what conflicts can occur.

### Evaluation Questions

* What story problem does this worldbuilding element create?
* What opportunity does it create?
* Who benefits from the system?
* Who suffers from it?
* How does it affect everyday life?
* Could the story take place almost unchanged in another setting?

If the answer to the last question is yes, consider whether the setting is doing enough work.

---

# 18. SCENE POTENTIAL

### Question

Does the idea immediately suggest memorable scenes?

Before strongly recommending a major story direction, mentally generate at least **five potential scenes** that become possible because of it.

For major premises, try to imagine ten.

These scenes do not need to appear in the final dossier.

The exercise is a test of dramatic productivity.

### Strong Signs

The idea naturally suggests:

* confrontations;
* discoveries;
* reversals;
* difficult choices;
* visually memorable situations;
* emotional interactions;
* failures;
* victories;
* escalating complications.

### Warning Sign

If an idea sounds fascinating when summarized but produces few actual scenes, it may be better suited to backstory, lore, or a short concept than a full novel.

---

# 19. ENDING POTENTIAL

### Question

Does the premise suggest a meaningful climax and resolution?

A strong story idea should contain the seeds of its ending.

The climax should ideally test:

* the protagonist's goal;
* their relationships;
* their values;
* what they have learned;
* what they are willing to sacrifice.

### Evaluation Questions

* What final choice could this story force?
* What confrontation does the premise promise?
* How might the climax test the protagonist differently from the opening?
* Can the external and internal conflicts resolve through the same decision?
* Does the ending have the potential to feel inevitable in retrospect but uncertain beforehand?

---

# 20. COMPRESSION TEST

Before considering a story concept well developed, attempt to reduce it to:

**Protagonist + Goal + Opposition + Stakes + Distinctive Complication**

Example:

> A disgraced dragon hunter must protect the last surviving dragon from the kingdom that once employed her, or her daughter will inherit the magical disease caused by the dragons' extinction.

If the story cannot be compressed clearly, determine whether:

* the protagonist lacks a clear goal;
* opposition is vague;
* stakes are unclear;
* the premise contains too many competing story engines;
* the distinctive hook has not yet been identified.

---

# OVERALL VIABILITY SCORE

Score each category from 1–5:

* Premise Clarity
* Narrative Engine
* Protagonist Agency
* Character Pressure
* Opposition
* Escalation Potential
* Emotional Stakes
* Character Arc Potential
* Relationship Potential
* Conflict Layers
* Cause-and-Effect Potential
* Irony and Contradiction
* Freshness
* Specificity
* Genre Promise
* Thematic Potential
* World/Story Integration
* Scene Potential
* Ending Potential
* Compression

Maximum possible score: **100**

Use the total only as a diagnostic aid.

### 85–100 — Exceptional Foundation

The story has a highly productive central engine with strong character, conflict, and plot possibilities.

Continue developing while protecting the core elements responsible for the high score.

### 70–84 — Strong Foundation

The concept is clearly viable.

Identify the lowest-scoring categories and strengthen them before adding unnecessary complexity.

### 55–69 — Promising but Underdeveloped

The story contains worthwhile elements, but several components may still be generic, disconnected, or insufficiently developed.

Continue brainstorming before treating the premise as settled.

### 40–54 — Structural Problems

The concept may contain a strong hook or individual elements, but the current version is unlikely to sustain the intended story without major development.

Identify whether the problem lies primarily in character, conflict, narrative engine, or stakes.

### Below 40 — Reconsider the Core Approach

Do not automatically discard the idea.

Determine whether there is one especially strong component worth rebuilding around.

Sometimes a weak story contains an excellent character, setting, conflict, or hook that should become the foundation of a substantially different version.

---

# WEAK-LINK RULE

Do not rely only on the total score.

Some categories function as structural weak links.

Pay special attention when any of the following score **2 or lower**:

* Narrative Engine
* Protagonist Agency
* Opposition
* Escalation Potential
* Emotional Stakes
* Cause-and-Effect Potential
* Scene Potential
* Ending Potential

A story with excellent worldbuilding and originality may still fail if its protagonist has nothing meaningful to do.

When one of these areas is weak, prioritize solving it before adding more lore, characters, twists, or subplots.

---

# MULTIPLIER RULE

Some decisions strengthen several categories simultaneously.

Favor these whenever possible.

For example:

A protagonist's estranged sister becomes the antagonist.

This choice may simultaneously improve:

* opposition;
* emotional stakes;
* relationship potential;
* character pressure;
* thematic conflict;
* climax potential.

A strong brainstorming choice often solves **three or more story problems at once**.

When comparing alternatives, ask:

> Which option creates the most useful consequences elsewhere in the novel?

---

# COMPLICATION TEST

For any major suggestion, ask:

> Does this addition generate complications or merely information?

Information:

> The kingdom was founded 500 years ago.

Complication:

> The kingdom's rulers must surrender one child to the neighboring empire every generation in exchange for peace.

The second detail creates choices, politics, relationships, resentment, and future events.

Prioritize **consequence-generating details**.

---

# WHY THIS PERSON? TEST

For every protagonist, ask:

> Why is this story happening to this particular person rather than anyone else?

Strong answers connect several elements:

* history;
* personality;
* abilities;
* relationships;
* worldview;
* status;
* mistakes;
* vulnerabilities.

Also ask:

> Why does this story happen now?

If either answer is weak, look for a stronger connection between protagonist and conflict.

---

# STORY-VERSUS-LORE TEST

When brainstorming speculative, historical, or highly developed settings, distinguish between information that is interesting and information that produces story.

Ask:

> If this detail were removed, would character choices, conflict, or plot materially change?

If not, it may still be useful atmosphere or background, but it should not receive priority during early story development.

---

# ANTI-GENERIC FILTER

Whenever proposing a familiar element, do not stop at the label.

Do not treat the following as finished ideas:

* chosen one;
* prophecy;
* rebellion;
* secret organization;
* evil empire;
* corrupt corporation;
* magical academy;
* forbidden romance;
* revenge;
* ancient artifact;
* secret identity;
* lost heir;
* family secret;
* monster hunter.

Instead determine:

* What specifically makes this version different?
* What consequences does it create?
* Why is it necessary to this story?
* How does it interact with the protagonist?
* What scenes become possible because of it?

Familiar tropes are acceptable and often desirable.

Generic execution is the problem.

---

# OPTION COMPARISON METHOD

When offering the user several possible story directions, make the options **meaningfully different**.

Do not provide five cosmetic variations.

Evaluate each option using the viability rubric.

Then consider:

### Which option provides the strongest narrative engine?

### Which creates the greatest character pressure?

### Which produces the strongest emotional stakes?

### Which creates the most interesting relationships?

### Which offers the best escalation?

### Which feels freshest while still satisfying the genre?

### Which generates the most scenes?

### Which creates the strongest eventual climax?

If one option clearly outperforms the others, recommend it and explain why.

Do not pretend all options are equally strong.

---

# FINAL STORY VIABILITY CHECK

Before considering the core story concept ready for a full dossier, confirm that the following statements are substantially true:

* The protagonist is identifiable.
* The protagonist wants something specific.
* Something meaningful prevents them from getting it.
* The protagonist makes decisions that affect later events.
* The conflict can escalate.
* Failure carries emotional consequences.
* The central situation can generate enough scenes for a novel.
* Important relationships can change.
* The protagonist's internal life intersects with the external conflict.
* The world or setting affects what can happen.
* The premise contains at least one specific or distinctive element.
* Major events can follow through cause and effect.
* The midpoint can meaningfully change the situation.
* The story suggests a strong crisis or climax.
* The ending can demonstrate meaningful change.
* The concept satisfies the fundamental promise of its genre.

If several of these are still unclear, continue brainstorming rather than prematurely finalizing the dossier.

---

# PRIMARY DECISION RULE

When choosing between two workable ideas, favor the one that:

1. creates more conflict;
2. forces harder character choices;
3. affects more relationships;
4. creates stronger consequences;
5. produces more scenes;
6. improves escalation;
7. connects external plot with internal character development;
8. creates a more satisfying eventual climax;
9. strengthens the genre experience;
10. accomplishes several of these things simultaneously.

The goal is not to make the story more complicated.

The goal is to make its elements **more interconnected**.
