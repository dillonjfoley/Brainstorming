# CAUSE-AND-EFFECT PLOTTING

Use this framework to design, evaluate, and strengthen the causal structure of a novel.

A strong plot is not merely a sequence of interesting things that happen.

It is a chain in which:

* choices create consequences;
* consequences create new problems;
* new problems force new choices;
* revelations change strategy;
* relationships alter decisions;
* earlier actions return later.

The central question is:

> Why does this event happen because of what came before?

A plot becomes stronger when the answer is clear.

---

# 1. THE CORE CAUSALITY PRINCIPLE

Weak plotting often sounds like:

> This happens.

> Then this happens.

> Then this happens.

Strong plotting sounds like:

> This happens, therefore the protagonist does this.

> But that creates this problem.

> Therefore the protagonist must do this next.

The most useful test is:

**BUT / THEREFORE**

instead of:

**AND THEN**

---

# 2. “AND THEN” PLOTTING

“And then” plotting means events are connected mainly by chronology.

Example:

> Maya steals the artifact.

> And then assassins attack her.

> And then she travels to another city.

> And then she meets the prince.

These events may all be interesting.

But the reader may not feel that one causes the next.

The story can feel episodic.

---

# 3. “BUT / THEREFORE” PLOTTING

Stronger:

> Maya steals the artifact, **therefore** the assassins identify her.

> She flees to the only city outside their jurisdiction, **but** entering requires sponsorship from the prince she publicly humiliated years ago.

Now:

* theft causes pursuit;
* pursuit causes travel;
* travel causes relationship conflict.

The plot is interconnected.

---

# 4. CAUSALITY VS. COINCIDENCE

Coincidence is not automatically bad.

Coincidence can begin a story.

Example:

> The protagonist happens to witness a murder.

But after the story begins, rely increasingly on:

* decisions;
* motives;
* consequences;
* systems;
* established rules.

A useful principle is:

> Coincidence may create trouble. Character choices should usually determine how trouble develops.

---

# 5. EVENT CHAIN

For each major event, identify:

**Cause**
What creates the event?

**Event**
What happens?

**Immediate Consequence**
What changes immediately?

**Decision**
How does a character respond?

**New Condition**
What becomes true because of that decision?

This creates causal flow.

---

# 6. BASIC CAUSAL CHAIN

Use:

**Cause → Event → Choice → Consequence → New Problem**

Example:

Cause:

> protagonist discovers illegal records.

Event:

> supervisor orders them destroyed.

Choice:

> protagonist secretly copies them.

Consequence:

> supervisor discovers the copy exists.

New Problem:

> protagonist becomes target of internal investigation.

The plot grows from action.

---

# 7. CHARACTER CHOICE AS CAUSE

Whenever possible, major events should be caused by characters.

Ask:

> Who chose something that made this happen?

Possible causes:

* protagonist;
* antagonist;
* ally;
* rival;
* authority;
* supporting character.

This gives the cast agency.

---

# 8. PROTAGONIST-CAUSED PLOT

The protagonist should create important future problems through earlier choices.

Example:

They lie to protect a friend.

Therefore:

* investigator becomes suspicious;
* friend becomes dependent on lie;
* antagonist gains leverage;
* later truth becomes more damaging.

One choice generates several future scenes.

---

# 9. ANTAGONIST-CAUSED PLOT

The antagonist should also act causally.

Example:

Antagonist destroys evidence.

Therefore:

> protagonist needs witness.

Protagonist finds witness.

Therefore:

> antagonist discredits witness.

Opposition becomes active.

---

# 10. SUPPORTING CHARACTERS AS CAUSAL AGENTS

Do not make supporting characters exist only to react.

A friend can:

* reveal secret;
* choose rival;
* betray promise;
* make mistake;
* protect protagonist.

Their actions should sometimes redirect the plot.

---

# 11. WORLD RULES AS CAUSES

Established rules can also create causality.

Example:

Rule:

> using magic leaves a visible mark.

Protagonist uses magic secretly.

Therefore:

> authorities identify them.

The consequence feels earned because the rule existed before the event.

---

# 12. SYSTEMIC CAUSALITY

Institutions should respond logically.

Example:

Protagonist violates policy.

Therefore:

* access revoked;
* investigation begins;
* supervisor pressured;
* allies become cautious.

Systems create consequences.

---

# 13. RELATIONSHIP CAUSALITY

Relationships should affect decisions.

Example:

Protagonist distrusts partner.

Therefore:

> withholds evidence.

Partner discovers secrecy.

Therefore:

> begins independent investigation.

Relationship conflict becomes plot.

---

# 14. INTERNAL CAUSALITY

Psychology can also cause external events.

Example:

Fear:

> failure.

Therefore:

> protagonist refuses risky solution.

That delay allows antagonist to gain advantage.

Internal flaw becomes plot cause.

---

# 15. WANT AS CAUSAL DRIVER

The protagonist’s Want should cause action.

Want:

> win promotion.

Therefore:

> takes risky assignment.

Assignment:

> exposes corruption.

Now character desire launches story.

---

# 16. FEAR AS CAUSAL DRIVER

Fear may cause avoidance.

Fear:

> abandonment.

Therefore:

> protagonist hides truth from partner.

That secrecy creates the breakup they feared.

The Fear becomes self-fulfilling.

---

# 17. MISBELIEF AS CAUSAL DRIVER

A Misbelief should affect decisions.

Misbelief:

> authority cannot be trusted.

Therefore:

> protagonist refuses police help.

That decision isolates them.

Now the character arc has causal weight.

---

# 18. CONSEQUENCE SHOULD PERSIST

Do not allow important consequences to disappear.

If the protagonist:

* lies;
* steals;
* betrays;
* injures;
* promises;
* reveals;

the story should remember.

Ask:

> What remains true because this happened?

---

# 19. CONSEQUENCE MEMORY

Characters should remember earlier events.

Example:

If one character betrays another, later scenes should reflect:

* distrust;
* resentment;
* changed strategy;
* reduced access.

Avoid emotional resets.

---

# 20. PLOT MEMORY

The world should remember too.

Example:

Protagonist publicly accuses senator.

Later:

* media coverage;
* legal pressure;
* political allies react.

Public actions have public consequences.

---

# 21. DELAYED CONSEQUENCE

Not every consequence needs to appear immediately.

Early:

> protagonist falsifies signature.

Much later:

> signature is discovered during audit.

Delayed consequences can create satisfying callbacks.

---

# 22. HIDDEN CONSEQUENCE

A choice may create a consequence the protagonist does not initially know about.

Example:

They save one prisoner.

Unknown consequence:

> prison security changes for everyone.

Later:

> rescuing another becomes harder.

This adds realism.

---

# 23. UNINTENDED CONSEQUENCE

Good choices can create bad outcomes.

Example:

Protagonist exposes corrupt official.

Therefore:

> power vacuum allows worse official to rise.

This creates complexity.

---

# 24. INTENDED VS. ACTUAL RESULT

For important actions identify:

**Intended Result:**
What did the character expect?

**Actual Result:**
What happened?

The difference often creates story.

---

# 25. PARTIAL SUCCESS

An action may succeed in one way and fail in another.

Example:

Goal:

> rescue witness.

Success:

> witness survives.

Failure:

> witness refuses to testify.

Partial success is useful because it changes the situation without ending the engine.

---

# 26. SUCCESS CREATES CONSEQUENCE

Success should sometimes create a new problem.

Example:

The protagonist gets the promotion.

Therefore:

> now has to supervise former friend.

Victory generates interpersonal conflict.

---

# 27. FAILURE CREATES CONSEQUENCE

Failure should not mean merely:

> try again.

Example:

The rescue fails.

Therefore:

> hostage is moved and antagonist knows someone is coming.

The next attempt must be different.

---

# 28. CHAIN REACTION

One choice can cause multiple effects.

Example:

Protagonist leaks secret.

Consequences:

* employer fires them;
* sibling feels betrayed;
* journalist contacts them;
* antagonist realizes evidence exists.

One action branches into several plot lines.

---

# 29. BRANCHING CAUSALITY

Not every cause produces one consequence.

Use:

**One Cause → Several Effects**

Then:

**Several Effects → One Major Collision**

This creates interconnected plotting.

---

# 30. CONVERGING CAUSALITY

Different plot threads can converge.

Example:

Relationship subplot:

> partner loses trust.

Investigation plot:

> protagonist needs partner’s testimony.

Systemic plot:

> court deadline approaches.

All three collide at crisis.

This creates structural density.

---

# 31. PLOT THREAD INTERDEPENDENCE

Subplots should affect main plot.

Ask:

> What does this subplot cause?

If the answer is:

> nothing outside itself,

it may feel detachable.

---

# 32. SUBPLOT CAUSALITY

A subplot can:

* create resource;
* remove ally;
* reveal information;
* change protagonist;
* alter stakes.

Make subplot events matter elsewhere.

---

# 33. RELATIONSHIP SUBPLOT CAUSALITY

Example:

Romantic partner learns protagonist lied.

Therefore:

> refuses to provide access needed for investigation.

Relationship and plot now interact.

---

# 34. CAUSAL ESCALATION

Each consequence should make later choices harder.

Example:

Lie → distrust → separation → lack of help → failed mission.

The story escalates because earlier decisions accumulate.

---

# 35. CAUSAL TURNING POINTS

Major plot turns should grow from prior events.

Weak midpoint:

> unexpected stranger reveals secret.

Stronger midpoint:

> antagonist exposes secret after protagonist’s earlier lie creates evidence.

The turn is earned.

---

# 36. INCITING INCIDENT CAUSALITY

The inciting incident may be partly coincidental.

But ask:

> What existing condition makes this event matter specifically to this protagonist?

This links setup to story.

---

# 37. COMMITMENT CAUSALITY

The commitment should arise from:

* inciting incident;
* protagonist Want;
* Fear;
* relationship;
* obligation.

Avoid:

> protagonist joins because plot requires it.

---

# 38. MIDPOINT CAUSALITY

Ask:

> What choices in the first half make this midpoint possible?

A good midpoint should not feel imported from another story.

---

# 39. CRISIS CAUSALITY

The crisis should result from accumulated choices.

Example:

Protagonist repeatedly chooses family over law.

Crisis:

> must finally choose whether to destroy evidence.

The dilemma has been built.

---

# 40. CLIMAX CAUSALITY

The climax should be the natural consequence of:

* protagonist choices;
* antagonist strategy;
* established rules;
* relationships;
* stakes.

Avoid last-minute mechanics.

---

# 41. CAUSAL FORESHADOWING

Foreshadowing should establish possibility.

Example:

Early:

> bridge has structural weakness.

Later:

> collapse matters.

The earlier setup does not need to announce the payoff.

---

# 42. SETUP → PRESSURE → PAYOFF

A strong payoff often uses an element that appeared earlier.

Example:

Setup:

> protagonist cannot swim.

Pressure:

> must cross flooded tunnel.

Payoff:

> trusts ally to guide them.

This can also serve character arc.

---

# 43. PLANT AND PAYOFF

Useful plants include:

* skill;
* object;
* rule;
* promise;
* fear;
* relationship;
* piece of information.

Ask:

> What later event could grow from this?

---

# 44. CHEKHOV PRINCIPLE

If something receives unusual emphasis, readers expect it may matter.

Use that expectation deliberately.

Do not make every detail a plot device.

But avoid spotlighting meaningless elements.

---

# 45. CAUSAL REVEAL

A revelation should explain earlier events.

Example:

Reveal:

> mentor sabotaged mission.

This should clarify:

* earlier missing equipment;
* strange advice;
* delayed rescue.

Revelations become satisfying when they reorganize prior information.

---

# 46. RETROACTIVE CAUSALITY

A later reveal can change the meaning of an earlier scene.

Example:

Earlier:

> father refuses to help.

Later:

> discover he was trying to keep protagonist away from danger.

The event remains the same.

Its interpretation changes.

---

# 47. FAIRNESS TEST

For reveals and twists ask:

> Could the reader understand afterward how this happened?

If yes, the twist feels earned.

If no, it may feel arbitrary.

---

# 48. SURPRISE VS. LOGIC

Prefer:

> surprising outcome produced by established causes

over:

> unpredictable outcome with no setup.

The best turn is:

> I didn’t expect that, but now it makes sense.

---

# 49. CAUSAL CHARACTER ARC

The character arc should also follow causality.

Example:

Fear creates behavior.

Behavior causes consequence.

Consequence creates pressure.

Pressure creates realization.

Realization creates new choice.

Arc is not separate from plot.

---

# 50. BEHAVIOR → CONSEQUENCE → CHANGE

Example:

Protagonist refuses help.

Therefore:

> mission fails.

Failure:

> harms ally.

Therefore:

> protagonist recognizes cost of isolation.

Later:

> chooses trust.

This is character-driven causality.

---

# 51. CAUSAL RELATIONSHIP ARC

Relationships should change because of behavior.

Trust should not rise or fall randomly.

Example:

A sacrifices safety for B.

Therefore:

> B trusts A more.

A later lies.

Therefore:

> trust fractures.

Relationship state has causes.

---

# 52. TRUST CAUSALITY

Identify why trust changes.

Trust increases through:

* reliability;
* honesty;
* vulnerability;
* sacrifice.

Trust decreases through:

* lies;
* betrayal;
* neglect;
* manipulation.

Use specific causes.

---

# 53. CAUSAL ANTAGONIST ARC

Antagonist escalation should also make sense.

Example:

Protagonist embarrasses antagonist publicly.

Therefore:

> antagonist stops using quiet pressure and becomes aggressive.

Opposition changes because of conflict.

---

# 54. CAUSAL WORLD CHANGE

Large-scale changes should come from established structures.

Example:

King dies.

Therefore:

> succession rules activate.

Factions respond according to existing incentives.

The world feels systemic rather than arbitrary.

---

# 55. CAUSALITY THROUGH INCENTIVES

Ask:

> Why would this character choose this?

Strong answers involve:

* desire;
* fear;
* values;
* incentives;
* pressure.

Avoid making characters act irrationally solely to move plot.

---

# 56. MOTIVATION CHAIN

For major action:

**Character Wants X → Believes Y → Therefore Chooses Z**

Example:

Wants:

> protect daughter.

Believes:

> police cannot be trusted.

Therefore:

> hides evidence.

The decision becomes understandable.

---

# 57. CAUSAL MISTAKES

Characters may make bad decisions.

That is fine.

But the mistake should arise from:

* limited information;
* fear;
* bias;
* urgency;
* flaw.

Avoid obviously foolish action with no explanation.

---

# 58. REASONABLE WRONG DECISION

Strong plots often use decisions that make sense in the moment but later cause trouble.

Example:

Protagonist destroys evidence to protect brother.

Later:

> evidence was needed to prove brother innocent.

The mistake feels tragic rather than stupid.

---

# 59. INFORMATION-BASED CAUSALITY

Characters act based on what they know.

Track:

* what they know;
* what they think they know;
* what they do not know.

This helps maintain logic.

---

# 60. INFORMATION STATE

For major turns ask:

> What information does each character have at this point?

Then:

> What decision does that information justify?

This prevents convenient behavior.

---

# 61. MISINFORMATION CAUSALITY

False information can cause events.

Example:

Character believes:

> ally betrayed them.

Therefore:

> refuses help.

Later:

> learns evidence was planted.

The false belief still caused real consequences.

---

# 62. SECRET CAUSALITY

Secrets should affect behavior before they are revealed.

If someone is secretly guilty:

* they avoid topics;
* manipulate evidence;
* act defensively.

The secret should already be exerting causal pressure.

---

# 63. BACKSTORY CAUSALITY

Backstory matters when it influences present choices.

Weak:

> detailed childhood that never affects action.

Strong:

> past betrayal causes current refusal to trust.

Backstory should be causal, not decorative.

---

# 64. GHOST CAUSALITY

A formative event may create:

Ghost → Misbelief → Strategy → Choice → Consequence.

This connects past to present.

---

# 65. THE DOMINO TEST

Imagine each major event as a domino.

Ask:

> If I remove this event, do later events still happen the same way?

If yes, the event may not be causally important.

Strong events knock over later dominos.

---

# 66. THE REMOVAL TEST

For any major scene ask:

> If this scene disappeared, what breaks?

Possible answers:

* later information missing;
* relationship state changes;
* plan becomes impossible;
* consequence never occurs.

If nothing breaks, the scene may be optional.

---

# 67. THE REORDERING TEST

Ask:

> Could these scenes occur in almost any order?

If yes, causality may be weak.

Strong sequences often have a necessary order.

---

# 68. NECESSARY ORDER

Example:

1. Protagonist lies.
2. Partner discovers lie.
3. Partner withdraws.
4. Protagonist lacks help.
5. Mission fails.

Changing order breaks logic.

That is causal plotting.

---

# 69. EPISODIC WARNING

A story may feel episodic if each problem begins and ends independently.

Example:

Chapter 1:

> monster.

Chapter 2:

> unrelated monster.

Chapter 3:

> unrelated monster.

Strengthen by linking:

> monster attacks are consequences of one evolving problem.

---

# 70. QUEST EPISODIC WARNING

Travel stories are especially vulnerable.

Avoid:

> arrive at place → face obstacle → leave → repeat.

Instead make each stop change:

* resources;
* knowledge;
* relationships;
* goal.

---

# 71. PROCEDURAL EPISODIC WARNING

Mysteries and procedurals can also become repetitive.

Each investigation step should:

* eliminate theory;
* reveal motive;
* create suspect;
* damage relationship;
* increase danger.

The case should evolve.

---

# 72. CAUSAL SCENE DESIGN

For each scene ask:

**Because of what previous event does this scene happen?**

**What does the viewpoint character want?**

**What happens because of the scene?**

If either causal connection is missing, strengthen it.

---

# 73. SCENE ENTRY CAUSE

A scene should usually begin because:

* someone needs something;
* earlier event requires response;
* deadline arrives;
* consequence appears.

Avoid scenes that exist only because author wants characters to talk.

---

# 74. SCENE EXIT CONSEQUENCE

At the end of a scene, something should change:

* decision;
* knowledge;
* trust;
* plan;
* stakes;
* location;
* resource.

This change helps cause the next scene.

---

# 75. SCENE-TO-SCENE BRIDGE

Use:

> Because this happened, the next scene must happen.

Example:

Scene 1:

> protagonist steals file.

Therefore Scene 2:

> security questions employees.

Therefore Scene 3:

> protagonist asks friend for alibi.

The sequence flows.

---

# 76. SEQUEL / RESPONSE SCENES

Not every scene needs immediate action.

After major event, characters may:

* process;
* decide;
* plan;
* grieve.

But response scenes should still produce:

> a new decision.

That decision causes the next action.

---

# 77. ACTION / REACTION CAUSALITY

Useful rhythm:

**Action → Consequence → Reaction → Decision → New Action**

This prevents breathless plotting without reflection.

---

# 78. CAUSAL PACING

Causality does not require constant speed.

A quiet scene can be causally essential if it changes:

* commitment;
* relationship;
* plan.

Do not confuse momentum with action.

---

# 79. CAUSAL TENSION

Readers anticipate consequences.

If protagonist lies, the reader may wonder:

> When will this be discovered?

Cause creates future tension before payoff.

---

# 80. CAUSAL PROMISE

An action creates expectation.

Example:

Protagonist accepts dangerous favor.

Reader expects:

> repayment.

Delayed payoff creates suspense.

---

# 81. CAUSAL DEBT

Earlier help creates future obligation.

Use:

> favor now → cost later.

This makes relationships and plot interconnected.

---

# 82. CAUSAL LOOP

Sometimes consequences reinforce the behavior that caused them.

Example:

Protagonist distrusts others.

Therefore:

> acts alone.

Others feel excluded.

Therefore:

> withhold information.

Protagonist sees this as proof others cannot be trusted.

This creates a self-reinforcing loop.

---

# 83. BREAKING THE LOOP

Character growth may require breaking causal cycle.

Example:

Old:

Fear → Control → Resentment → Abandonment → More Fear.

Growth:

Fear → Trust → Cooperation → Different Outcome.

This makes the arc visible in plot.

---

# 84. CAUSAL IRONY

The protagonist may cause the very outcome they try to prevent.

Examples:

Fear of losing child:

> becomes controlling.

Control:

> drives child away.

This creates dramatic irony.

---

# 85. TRAGIC CAUSALITY

In tragedy, the protagonist’s strengths or flaws may cause downfall.

Example:

Loyalty causes them to protect guilty friend.

Protection leads to larger crime.

Downfall feels inevitable.

---

# 86. CAUSAL COMEDY

Comedy can also use causality.

Small lie:

→ larger lie

→ absurd cover-up

→ social disaster.

Escalating consequences generate humor.

---

# 87. ROMANCE CAUSALITY

Relationship development should be caused by shared experiences and choices.

Example:

Forced collaboration:

→ respect

→ vulnerability

→ intimacy

→ fear

→ withdrawal

→ crisis.

Avoid romance advancing only because the outline says it is time.

---

# 88. MYSTERY CAUSALITY

Clues should lead to:

* questions;
* suspects;
* theories;
* actions.

Example:

Clue:

> unusual receipt.

Therefore:

> detective visits store.

There:

> learns victim met someone.

That creates new suspect.

The investigation grows causally.

---

# 89. THRILLER CAUSALITY

Thrillers benefit from:

Move → Countermove → Escalation.

Avoid random danger.

The antagonist should attack because of what protagonist did.

---

# 90. HORROR CAUSALITY

Horror can feel inevitable when:

* curiosity opens door;
* denial delays escape;
* rule is broken;
* safety deteriorates.

The horror responds to behavior.

---

# 91. FANTASY CAUSALITY

World rules should create consequences.

Example:

Magic requires blood.

Therefore:

> using it leaves physical evidence.

Evidence:

> reveals protagonist.

Fantasy logic supports plot logic.

---

# 92. SCIENCE FICTION CAUSALITY

The speculative premise should produce downstream effects.

Technology exists.

Therefore:

* society changes;
* incentives change;
* behavior changes;
* conflict becomes possible.

Do not use technology only as decoration.

---

# 93. CAUSAL POLITICS

Political stories require actors responding to incentives.

Example:

King dies.

Therefore:

* heir claims throne;
* rival faction mobilizes;
* foreign power intervenes;
* general must choose side.

Political events should not happen arbitrarily.

---

# 94. FACTION CAUSALITY

Each faction should ask:

> What action best serves our interests now?

Then act.

Their decisions create dynamic world conflict.

---

# 95. CAUSALITY AND ESCALATION

The strongest escalation comes from prior action.

Weak:

> situation gets worse because more enemies arrive.

Stronger:

> more enemies arrive because protagonist publicly challenged the organization.

The protagonist helped create escalation.

---

# 96. CAUSALITY AND STAKES

Choices can endanger what the protagonist values.

Example:

Investigating conspiracy:

> puts spouse’s job at risk.

The stakes grow from pursuit.

---

# 97. CAUSALITY AND CONFLICT LAYERS

One action can affect multiple layers.

Example:

Protagonist leaks report.

External:

> investigation advances.

Interpersonal:

> partner feels betrayed.

Internal:

> guilt increases.

Systemic:

> institution retaliates.

One cause creates layered conflict.

---

# 98. CAUSALITY AND RELATIONSHIP ARCHITECTURE

What characters want from each other should affect decisions.

Example:

A wants forgiveness from B.

Therefore:

> hides evidence that would hurt B.

This changes plot.

---

# 99. CAUSALITY AND CHARACTER FUNCTION

Every major character should be capable of causing something.

Ask:

> What event exists because this character chose to act?

If answer is none, their function may be weak.

---

# 100. CAUSALITY AND THEMATIC ARGUMENT

Theme becomes stronger when different values cause different outcomes.

Example:

Character A chooses control.

Outcome:

> alienation.

Character B chooses trust.

Outcome:

> cooperation.

Plot tests ideas.

---

# 101. CAUSALITY AND WORLD BUILDING

Worldbuilding should create:

* incentives;
* restrictions;
* opportunities;
* consequences.

Ask:

> What happens because this world works this way?

---

# 102. CAUSALITY AND INFORMATION REVEAL

A revelation should change:

* plan;
* trust;
* stakes;
* goal.

If nothing changes, it may be trivia.

---

# 103. CAUSALITY AND MIDPOINT

A midpoint should alter second-half causality.

Example:

First half:

> investigate murder.

Midpoint:

> discover victim wanted to be killed.

Second half:

> investigate motive and conspiracy.

New information changes what actions logically follow.

---

# 104. CAUSALITY AND CRISIS

The crisis should be built from prior consequences.

Example:

Because protagonist protected brother,

evidence was destroyed.

Because evidence was destroyed,

innocent person may be convicted.

Now protagonist must decide whether to confess.

The crisis is earned.

---

# 105. CAUSALITY AND CLIMAX

The climax should pay off multiple causal chains.

Example:

Earlier:

* protagonist learned rule;
* relationship built trust;
* sacrifice established value;
* antagonist created weakness.

Climax uses all four.

This creates satisfaction.

---

# 106. CAUSAL PAYOFF DENSITY

A strong climax often resolves several earlier setups at once.

Favor:

> one action with multiple payoffs

over:

> many unrelated solutions.

---

# 107. CAUSAL ECONOMY

Prefer one event that does several jobs.

Example:

Protagonist publicly confesses.

This:

* clears innocent suspect;
* ruins reputation;
* repairs relationship;
* exposes antagonist;
* fulfills character Need.

Efficient plotting feels cohesive.

---

# 108. CAUSAL REDUNDANCY

If two scenes cause the same outcome, consider combining them.

Example:

Scene A:

> protagonist learns antagonist is dangerous.

Scene B:

> protagonist learns antagonist is dangerous again.

Combine or differentiate consequences.

---

# 109. CAUSAL DEAD END

A plot thread is a dead end if it generates no future consequence.

Ask:

> What later event grows from this?

If none, either:

* remove it;
* use it for character/theme intentionally;
* connect it to another thread.

---

# 110. CAUSAL ORPHAN

An event is an orphan if nothing clearly caused it.

Example:

> suddenly a new villain arrives.

Ask:

> What earlier condition explains their arrival?

Connect them.

---

# 111. CAUSAL BRIDGE

When two events feel disconnected, insert a bridge.

Example:

Event A:

> hero loses job.

Event B:

> hero investigates murder.

Bridge:

> losing job forces hero to take private investigation work.

Now A causes B.

---

# 112. CAUSAL COMPRESSION

Try summarizing plot with “because.”

Example:

> Because Mara steals the records, the company investigates her; because she is investigated, she asks her brother to hide the files; because he hides them, he becomes legally implicated; because he is implicated, Mara must choose between exposing the conspiracy and protecting him.

If the summary works, causal structure is likely strong.

---

# 113. CAUSAL OUTLINE METHOD

For each major beat write:

**Because:**
What prior event caused this?

**Therefore:**
What happens next because of this?

If either side is blank, investigate.

---

# 114. BUT / THEREFORE OUTLINE

Write the story using only:

**BUT**

and

**THEREFORE**

Example:

The protagonist wants to rescue the prince,

**BUT** the prince refuses to leave.

**THEREFORE** the protagonist investigates why.

**BUT** discovers the prince is hiding from the queen.

**THEREFORE** protagonist confronts queen.

This reveals structural flow.

---

# 115. CAUSAL OUTLINE WARNING

If outline repeatedly uses:

* meanwhile;
* suddenly;
* coincidentally;
* meanwhile elsewhere;

check whether the plot is fragmenting.

These words are not forbidden.

They are warning signs when overused.

---

# 116. “MEANWHILE” TEST

Parallel plots can work.

But ask:

> When do these threads causally affect each other?

If never, they may feel like separate stories.

---

# 117. “SUDDENLY” TEST

Unexpected events can be exciting.

Ask:

> Was this event established as possible?

If no, it may feel arbitrary.

---

# 118. “COINCIDENTALLY” TEST

Coincidence should usually complicate rather than solve.

Better:

> protagonist accidentally sees antagonist.

This creates danger.

Weaker:

> protagonist accidentally finds exact evidence needed to win.

---

# 119. CAUSAL SOLUTION STANDARD

Solutions should come from:

* prior knowledge;
* skill;
* relationships;
* sacrifice;
* established rule.

Avoid convenient rescue.

---

# 120. CAUSAL PROBLEM STANDARD

Problems should come from:

* choices;
* opposition;
* world;
* consequences.

Avoid random complications used only to pad middle.

---

# 121. CAUSAL DENSITY

Strong scenes may be both:

> consequence of earlier events

and

> cause of later events.

This creates density.

---

# 122. THE DOUBLE-ARROW TEST

For each major scene ask:

> What points into this scene?

and

> What points out of it?

If both are strong, the scene is integrated.

---

# 123. SCENE CAUSALITY TEMPLATE

**Previous Cause:**
Why must this scene happen now?

**Character Goal:**
What does viewpoint character want?

**Action:**
What do they do?

**Resistance:**
What prevents easy success?

**Outcome:**
What happens?

**Consequence:**
What later event does this cause?

---

# 124. SEQUENCE CAUSALITY TEMPLATE

For a sequence of scenes:

1. Problem appears.
2. Character chooses tactic.
3. Tactic creates result.
4. Result changes conditions.
5. Character must adapt.
6. New problem emerges.

This keeps sequences from feeling repetitive.

---

# 125. PLOT THREAD TEMPLATE

For each subplot ask:

**Origin:**
What creates this thread?

**Development:**
What choices keep it moving?

**Intersection:**
Where does it affect main plot?

**Payoff:**
What changes because it existed?

---

# 126. CHARACTER CAUSALITY TEMPLATE

For each major character:

**What do they want?**

**What do they choose because of that Want?**

**What does that choice cause?**

**Who must respond?**

This helps ensure every major character generates story.

---

# 127. CAUSALITY QA

Before considering the plot well connected, confirm:

* Major events have understandable causes.
* Protagonist choices create future consequences.
* Antagonist actions provoke responses.
* Supporting characters sometimes redirect plot.
* Established world rules matter.
* Relationships affect decisions.
* Internal psychology affects behavior.
* Failures change conditions.
* Successes create new opportunities or complications.
* Earlier actions return later.
* Midpoint grows from first-half choices.
* Crisis grows from accumulated consequences.
* Climax pays off established elements.
* Coincidence does not routinely solve problems.
* Major scenes are both caused by and cause other scenes.

---

# 128. WEAK-LINK DIAGNOSTIC

### Interesting Events, Weak Causality

The outline may feel episodic.

Add stronger “therefore” links.

### Strong Plot, Weak Character Causality

Events happen logically, but protagonist feels passive.

Make their decisions create turns.

### Strong Character, Weak External Causality

Psychology is rich, but story wanders.

Connect internal choices to external consequences.

### Strong Reveals, Weak Consequences

Information does not change action.

Make revelations alter strategy.

### Strong Action, Weak Memory

Events occur but leave no residue.

Carry consequences forward.

---

# 129. CAUSAL MULTIPLIER RULE

Favor choices that create several consequences.

Example:

The protagonist lies in court.

This can cause:

* legal danger;
* relationship damage;
* antagonist leverage;
* internal guilt;
* future evidence contradiction.

One cause powers multiple threads.

---

# 130. CAUSAL ECONOMY RULE

Before adding a new event, ask:

> Can an earlier choice cause this instead?

Before adding a new character, ask:

> Can an existing relationship cause this?

Before adding a new twist, ask:

> Can an established rule or consequence produce it?

This makes the story feel tighter.

---

# 131. CAUSALITY OVER COMPLEXITY

A complex plot is not necessarily a strong plot.

A simple story with clear causes can feel more satisfying than a complicated story filled with twists.

Prioritize:

> connection

over:

> quantity.

---

# 132. CAUSALITY OVER SURPRISE

Do not choose a twist solely because it is unexpected.

Choose:

> the surprising outcome that best grows from established causes.

---

# 133. CAUSALITY OVER CONVENIENCE

When deciding how to solve a story problem, avoid:

> What could conveniently happen?

Ask:

> What would logically happen because of what these characters have already done?

---

# 134. CAUSALITY OVER AUTHORIAL NEED

Do not make a character behave a certain way because:

> the plot needs them to.

Instead ask:

> What motivation would naturally make them behave this way?

If no credible motivation exists, change the plot.

---

# 135. THE INEVITABILITY PRINCIPLE

The ideal ending should feel:

> surprising in execution

but

> inevitable in retrospect.

The reader should be able to trace:

* character choices;
* consequences;
* relationships;
* rules;
* revelations

all the way to the climax.

---

# 136. THE “COULD THIS HAVE GONE DIFFERENTLY?” TEST

Causality should not eliminate uncertainty.

Characters could have chosen differently.

That is what gives decisions weight.

A strong plot feels inevitable **because these particular characters made these particular choices**, not because no other outcome was possible.

---

# 137. CAUSAL CHARACTER SPECIFICITY

Ask:

> Would the same chain happen with a different protagonist?

If yes, the plot may not be personalized enough.

Their:

* Fear;
* Want;
* relationships;
* values;

should influence causality.

---

# 138. CAUSAL ANTAGONIST SPECIFICITY

Ask:

> Would any generic villain create the same plot?

If yes, strengthen antagonist strategy and motive.

---

# 139. CAUSAL WORLD SPECIFICITY

Ask:

> Could the story happen unchanged in another world?

If yes, worldbuilding may not be causally integrated.

---

# 140. CAUSAL RELATIONSHIP SPECIFICITY

Ask:

> Would these events happen if the characters were strangers?

If not, the relationship is doing useful causal work.

---

# 141. CAUSAL MIDPOINT TEST

At midpoint ask:

* Which earlier choice caused this?
* What does this new condition cause?
* What old strategy becomes impossible?
* What new decision becomes necessary?

A strong midpoint sits in the middle of a causal chain, not outside it.

---

# 142. CAUSAL CRISIS TEST

At crisis ask:

> Which earlier choices created this impossible dilemma?

If the dilemma could appear in any story without preparation, strengthen setup.

---

# 143. CAUSAL CLIMAX TEST

At climax ask:

* What earlier skill is used?
* What earlier relationship matters?
* What earlier sacrifice pays off?
* What earlier mistake returns?
* What established rule determines outcome?

The climax should be densely connected to the whole novel.

---

# 144. CAUSAL CLOSING IMAGE

The closing state should result from the climax.

Ask:

> Why is the protagonist here now?

The answer should trace back to their choices.

---

# 145. CAUSALITY AND DOSSIER DESIGN

When summarizing the story in a dossier, avoid listing plot points as isolated bullets.

Even if the final format is brief, make sure the underlying logic is:

* because;
* therefore;
* but;
* consequently.

The dossier should imply progression.

---

# 146. DOSSIER PLOT CHAIN

Before finalizing the dossier, internally summarize:

**Opening Condition**

↓ because of

**Inciting Incident**

↓ protagonist responds by

**Commitment**

↓ creates

**Major Complication**

↓ leads to

**Midpoint**

↓ causes

**Second-Half Consequences**

↓ forces

**Crisis**

↓ protagonist chooses

**Climax**

↓ results in

**Closing State**

If the arrows are difficult to explain, continue plotting.

---

# 147. ANTI-GENERIC CAUSALITY TEST

Avoid:

> villain attacks because villain is evil.

Ask:

> Why now?

Avoid:

> protagonist investigates because they are curious.

Ask:

> What personal consequence forces involvement?

Avoid:

> ally betrays protagonist because story needs twist.

Ask:

> What independent goal makes betrayal rational?

---

# 148. QUICK CAUSALITY TEST

For every major beat answer:

1. What caused this?
2. Who chose something?
3. Why did they choose it?
4. What changes because of it?
5. What new problem does it create?

If these answers are strong, the beat is probably integrated.

---

# 149. CAUSALITY RED-FLAG WORDS

Be cautious when an outline relies repeatedly on:

* suddenly;
* unexpectedly;
* by coincidence;
* fortunately;
* unfortunately;
* meanwhile;
* just then.

These words are not inherently bad.

They are prompts to check:

> Is a stronger causal connection possible?

---

# 150. FINAL CAUSALITY CHECK

Before finalizing the story, confirm:

* The inciting incident creates a meaningful response.
* Commitment arises from character choice.
* Early actions create later problems.
* The antagonist responds intelligently.
* Supporting characters generate consequences.
* World rules influence events.
* Relationship changes affect strategy.
* Internal fears cause behavior.
* Failures change future conditions.
* Successes create new consequences.
* Subplots intersect the main plot.
* Midpoint grows from earlier actions.
* Crisis results from accumulated choices.
* Climax uses established causes and payoffs.
* Resolution reflects the consequences of the climax.
* The story can largely be summarized using “but” and “therefore” rather than “and then.”

---

# PRIMARY CAUSE-AND-EFFECT RULE

Do not ask only:

> What happens next?

Ask:

> What happens because of what just happened?

Then:

> Who responds?

> What do they choose?

> What does that choice cost?

> What new condition does it create?

> What future problem is now inevitable?

The strongest plot is not a chain of events.

It is a chain of **consequences created by people trying to get what they want**.
